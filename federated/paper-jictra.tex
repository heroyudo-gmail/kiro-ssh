\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Federated learning (FL) lets organizations train a shared network intrusion
detection model without sharing raw traffic, but real deployments face
\emph{feature-schema heterogeneity}: clients extract features with different
tools, so their feature spaces differ. We study whether a validated Semantic
Feature Mapping (SFM) can bridge this gap and enable cross-dataset FL between
CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15. Using an identical lightweight model and FedAvg
across all settings, we run a controlled experiment that isolates the effect of
heterogeneity. On homogeneous partitions (one dataset split IID across clients)
FedAvg behaves normally, reaching Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) close to
the centralized bound (0.563 and 0.674). A graded non-IID sweep locates a sharp
break point: the balanced dataset (UNSW) collapses near Dirichlet
\(\alpha\approx0.1\), while the benign-skewed dataset (CIC) stays stable. In the
true cross-dataset setting, naive FedAvg diverges (global MCC \(-0.026\)) and a
one-class autoencoder variant does not recover it. We conclude that SFM is
necessary for interoperability but not sufficient for detection under label-prior
heterogeneity, and we quantify exactly when cross-dataset FL fails.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} \emph{federated learning; network intrusion
detection; cross-dataset generalization; semantic feature mapping; non-IID;
data heterogeneity; FedAvg.}

%==================================================================
\section{Introduction}
%==================================================================
Network Intrusion Detection Systems (NIDS) based on machine learning typically
assume centralized training, where traffic from many sources is pooled on a
single server. In cross-organization settings this assumption breaks down for two
reasons: privacy (sensitive traffic cannot leave an organization) and
\emph{feature-schema heterogeneity} (each organization extracts flow features with
a different tool, e.g.\ CICFlowMeter versus Argus/Bro, producing different feature
sets). Federated learning (FL) addresses privacy by exchanging only model
parameters, but standard FedAvg assumes an identical feature space across clients
\cite{mcmahan,ref-fl-survey}.

A Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) --- a statistically validated dictionary that
maps features from different extraction tools to a common semantic space --- has
been proposed to bridge schema heterogeneity for \emph{centralized} cross-dataset
generalization \cite{paper1}. A natural question follows: can SFM also serve as an
\emph{enabler} for federated learning across clients that use different feature
schemas, and how far does federated-SFM close the gap to a centralized model?

This paper answers that question with a \emph{controlled} empirical study rather
than a claim of superiority. Crucially, our setting departs from existing FL-NIDS
work in a specific way. Prior studies --- including state-of-the-art systems such
as Fed-ANIDS \cite{fedanids} --- partition a \emph{single} dataset across clients,
so all clients share one feature schema and a near-identical distribution. We
instead federate across \emph{two different-source datasets} (CIC and UNSW) whose
feature-extraction tools, feature schemas, and label priors all differ. This is
the harder, more realistic case that prior work avoids, and it is enabled only by
a validated semantic feature space. Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{A new problem setting:} cross-dataset FL-NIDS across clients with
  \emph{different feature schemas}, bridged by a validated Semantic Feature
  Mapping used as an interoperability enabler --- not a single dataset split, as
  in prior FL-NIDS work.
  \item \textbf{A controlled protocol} that holds the model and aggregation fixed
  and varies only the degree of heterogeneity, isolating its causal effect; a
  homogeneous control confirms the pipeline is valid (near-centralized MCC).
  \item \textbf{A quantified break point:} a graded non-IID sweep that locates
  where FedAvg collapses and explains the asymmetry between a balanced and a
  benign-skewed dataset --- an analysis not reported for cross-dataset FL-NIDS.
  \item \textbf{An honest, diagnosed negative result:} naive FedAvg diverges and a
  one-class autoencoder (the Fed-ANIDS paradigm) does not recover it; we show SFM
  is necessary for schema interoperability but not sufficient for detection under
  label-prior heterogeneity.
\end{enumerate}
To be explicit about scope: our novelty is in the \emph{problem framing, the
cross-schema federation via SFM, and the controlled when-does-it-fail analysis},
not in a new aggregation algorithm. We reuse standard FedAvg/FedProx deliberately,
so that the observed behavior reflects the data heterogeneity we study rather than
a bespoke optimizer.

%==================================================================
\section{Related Work}
%==================================================================
FL for intrusion detection has grown rapidly; recent surveys catalog
architectures, aggregation strategies, and open challenges
\cite{ref-fl-survey,ref-fl-review}. Most FL-NIDS studies evaluate on a
\emph{single} dataset split across clients, i.e.\ a homogeneous feature space and
near-IID distribution. Fed-ANIDS \cite{fedanids}, a representative
state-of-the-art, combines FL with anomaly detection using autoencoders
(including adversarial variants) and reaches near-centralized accuracy --- but on
single-source data partitioned to clients. The limits of FL under heterogeneity
are increasingly recognized \cite{ref-limits,ref-personalized}; proximal methods
such as FedProx \cite{fedprox} and personalization mitigate client drift but do
not eliminate it.

Table~\ref{tab:position} positions this work against representative FL-NIDS
studies along three axes that matter for our question: whether clients share one
feature schema, whether the data is single- or cross-source, and whether the
study isolates \emph{when} FL fails. To our knowledge, no prior FL-NIDS study
combines cross-schema federation (via a validated semantic mapping) with a
controlled break-point analysis across the homogeneous-to-cross-dataset spectrum.
That combination is the gap this paper fills.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\caption{Positioning against representative FL-NIDS work.}
\label{tab:position}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Study & Feature schema & Data source & Break-point analysis \\
\midrule
Fed-ANIDS \cite{fedanids}        & single & single-source split & no \\
FL-NIDS surveys \cite{ref-fl-survey,ref-fl-review} & single & single-source & no \\
Limits of FL \cite{ref-limits}   & single & single-source & partial \\
Personalized FL \cite{ref-personalized} & single & single-source & no \\
\textbf{This work}               & \textbf{different (SFM-bridged)} & \textbf{cross-dataset} & \textbf{yes (graded)} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Our work therefore differs on all three axes: clients use \emph{different}
feature schemas reconciled by SFM, the federation is \emph{cross-dataset}, and we
frame the study around \emph{when} FL fails rather than asserting that it wins.

\section{Method}
%==================================================================
\subsection{Semantic Feature Mapping (public prior)}
We reuse the SFM dictionary from \cite{paper1}: nine semantically aligned flow
features (duration, forward/backward packets, forward/backward bytes,
forward/backward mean packet length, source/destination load). The mapping is a
\emph{public prior} agreed before training --- a dictionary of column
correspondences and derivations validated statistically on each dataset
\emph{separately}. Each client applies the mapping to its own data locally; no raw
data is exchanged, preserving the privacy premise of FL.

\subsection{Model and aggregation (held fixed across all settings)}
To make every setting comparable (apple-to-apple), we use one lightweight
multilayer perceptron (MLP) over the nine SFM features
(\(9\!\to\!128\!\to\!64\!\to\!1\), ReLU, sigmoid output) and classic FedAvg:
\begin{equation}
w^{(t+1)} = \sum_{k=1}^{K}\frac{n_k}{n}\,w_k^{(t)},\qquad n=\sum_k n_k,
\end{equation}
where client \(k\) trains locally for \(E\) epochs. We also test FedProx (a
proximal term \(\tfrac{\mu}{2}\lVert w-w^{(t)}\rVert^2\)) and a one-class
autoencoder variant (Section~\ref{sec:oc}). Per-client z-score normalization is
local (privacy preserving). Hyperparameters are identical across settings
(\(R=100\) rounds, \(E=5\), learning rate \(3\times10^{-3}\)).

\subsection{Metric}
We report the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as the primary metric,
because it is robust to the class imbalance common in NIDS traffic, complemented
by recall, precision, F1 and balanced accuracy.

\section{Experimental Design}
%==================================================================
Two datasets act as clients: CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (CIC) \cite{cicids} and UNSW-NB15
(UNSW) \cite{unsw}, both mapped to the nine SFM features. Table~\ref{tab:data} summarizes the per-client
statistics after a stratified subsample of CIC to balance client sizes.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small

\label{tab:data}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
Client (split) & Total & Attack & Pos-rate \\
\midrule
UNSW (train) & 82{,}332  & 45{,}332 & 0.551 \\
UNSW (test)  & 175{,}341 & 119{,}341 & 0.681 \\
CIC (train)  & 100{,}000 & 16{,}929 & 0.169 \\
CIC (test)   & 486{,}979 & 82{,}443 & 0.169 \\
\bottomrule

We run four experiments, from homogeneous to heterogeneous:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Homogeneous (control).} One dataset split IID across \(K=4\)
  clients; evaluate on the same-dataset test set.
  \item \textbf{Graded non-IID.} One dataset split via Dirichlet(\(\alpha\)) on
  labels; sweep \(\alpha\) from 100 (near-IID) to 0.01 (extreme) to locate the
  break point.
  \item \textbf{Cross-dataset (core).} Two clients \(\{\text{CIC},\text{UNSW}\}\);
  evaluate on CIC-test, UNSW-test and a pooled GLOBAL-test.
  \item \textbf{One-class mitigation.} Autoencoder trained only on normal traffic,
  detection by reconstruction error (Section~\ref{sec:oc}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Results and Discussion}
%==================================================================

\subsection{Homogeneous control: FedAvg works}
Table~\ref{tab:homo} shows that on homogeneous IID partitions, FedAvg reaches MCC
close to the centralized bound for both datasets, and above local-only. The
convergence curves rise smoothly without divergence. This establishes that the
FL pipeline (MLP, FedAvg, SFM space) is valid: any later failure is attributable
to heterogeneity, not to an implementation defect.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\caption{Homogeneous IID control (MCC, same-dataset test).}
\label{tab:homo}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Dataset & Centralized & Federated-IID & Local-only (mean) \\
\midrule
CIC-only  & 0.619 & 0.563 & 0.558 \\
UNSW-only & 0.723 & 0.674 & 0.671 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Graded non-IID: locating the break point}
Table~\ref{tab:bridge} sweeps the label-heterogeneity level on a single dataset.
UNSW (balanced prior 0.55) shows a sharp break near \(\alpha\approx0.1\): MCC
falls from 0.69 to 0.34. CIC (benign-skewed prior 0.17) stays stable down to
\(\alpha=0.01\). The asymmetry is explained by the label prior: when
\(\alpha\) is small, a balanced dataset forces nearly single-class clients, whose
local models diverge and cancel under averaging; a benign-skewed dataset keeps a
benign majority on every client, so local models stay compatible.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\caption{Graded non-IID sweep (federated MCC vs Dirichlet $\alpha$).}
\label{tab:bridge}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccccccc@{}}
\toprule
Dataset & cent. & $\alpha$100 & $\alpha$10 & $\alpha$1 & $\alpha$0.5 & $\alpha$0.1 & $\alpha$0.05 \\
\midrule
CIC  & 0.619 & 0.562 & 0.557 & 0.575 & 0.569 & 0.581 & 0.577 \\
UNSW & 0.723 & 0.672 & 0.673 & 0.698 & 0.694 & \textbf{0.337} & \textbf{0.310} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Cross-dataset: naive FedAvg diverges}
Table~\ref{tab:cross} reports the core setting. Centralized reaches global MCC
0.745 and a single local model (UNSW) 0.614, but federated-SFM collapses to
\(-0.026\): worse than local-only and near random. The convergence curve rises to
about 0.27 by round 10 then decays into negative territory --- the classic
client-drift divergence of FedAvg under extreme heterogeneity. Across five seeds
federated-SFM is 0.229\(\pm\)0.184 versus local-only 0.612\(\pm\)0.003 (paired-$t$
\(p=0.010\)), i.e.\ federated is \emph{significantly worse}. The cross-dataset
case lies beyond the break point found in Table~\ref{tab:bridge}.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\caption{Cross-dataset CIC$\leftrightarrow$UNSW (MCC).}
\label{tab:cross}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Setting & CIC-test & UNSW-test & GLOBAL-test \\
\midrule
Centralized      & 0.612 & 0.732 & 0.745 \\
Local-only (UNSW)& $-0.004$ & 0.723 & 0.614 \\
Federated-SFM    & 0.532 & 0.616 & \textbf{$-0.026$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{One-class mitigation does not recover it}
\label{sec:oc}
Motivated by the label-prior diagnosis, we tested a one-class autoencoder
(trained only on normal traffic, detection by reconstruction error), the paradigm
behind Fed-ANIDS \cite{fedanids}. Table~\ref{tab:oc} shows that this does not
recover cross-dataset performance: federated-AE reaches only global MCC 0.039,
and even the centralized autoencoder fails (near-zero MCC). This indicates that
the nine SFM features --- optimized for cross-dataset \emph{interoperability} ---
do not provide enough \emph{separability} for reconstruction-based detection on
these datasets. The limitation is the feature space, not merely the aggregation.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering\small
\caption{One-class autoencoder, cross-dataset (MCC).}
\label{tab:oc}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
Setting & CIC-test & UNSW-test & GLOBAL-test \\
\midrule
Centralized-AE & $-0.070$ & $-0.042$ & $-0.003$ \\
Local-only-AE  & 0.153 & 0.014 & --- \\
Federated-AE   & 0.150 & 0.002 & 0.039 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Discussion}
The results form a coherent, honest picture. FL over the SFM space is valid
(homogeneous control) and tolerant of moderate non-IID, but it has a measurable
break point driven by label-prior heterogeneity. Cross-dataset FL lies beyond
that point, so naive FedAvg diverges, and switching to a one-class paradigm does
not help because the alignment-oriented SFM space lacks detection separability.
SFM is therefore \emph{necessary} for schema interoperability but \emph{not
sufficient} for federated detection across datasets. These findings motivate
heterogeneity-aware aggregation (clustered or personalized FL) and feature spaces
designed jointly for alignment \emph{and} separability --- directions for future
work.

%==================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==================================================================
We presented a controlled study of cross-dataset federated NIDS over a semantic
feature space. By holding the model and aggregation fixed and varying only
heterogeneity, we showed that FedAvg works on homogeneous data (MCC 0.563/0.674,
near-centralized), degrades past a measurable non-IID break point
(\(\alpha\approx0.1\) for the balanced dataset), and fails in the true
cross-dataset setting (global MCC \(-0.026\)); a one-class variant does not
recover it. The study quantifies \emph{when} cross-dataset FL for NIDS fails and
why, and argues that semantic feature mapping is necessary but not sufficient.
Future work targets heterogeneity-aware aggregation and detection-oriented
feature spaces, plus a real multi-node cloud deployment.
