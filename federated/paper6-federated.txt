TITLE
================================================================================
Federated Learning for Cross-Dataset Network Intrusion Detection: Evaluation
Protocol, Feature Richness, and Adversarial Robustness on a Cloud Testbed


AUTHORS
================================================================================
Hero Yudo Martono, Iwan Syarif, Ferry Astika Saputra
Politeknik Elektronika Negeri Surabaya (PENS), Surabaya, Indonesia
hero@pens.ac.id, iwanarif@pens.ac.id, ferryas@pens.ac.id


ABSTRACT
================================================================================
Federated learning (FL) lets organizations train a network intrusion detection
system (NIDS) collaboratively without centralizing sensitive traffic, but results
across the FL-NIDS literature are hard to compare because datasets, aggregation,
and evaluation protocols differ. This paper studies when cross-dataset FL-NIDS
actually works, using two dataset pairs: clients that share one extractor
(CIC-IDS2017 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018, 76 aligned features) and clients that use
different extractors (CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15, 9 aligned features). We
verify six representative claims on a uniform Amazon Web Services testbed and add
three contributions. First, a leakage-free evaluation protocol that normalizes
each test set with its owner client's training statistics; an inconsistent pooled
evaluation makes the same federated model look far worse (union Matthews
correlation coefficient, MCC, drops from 0.65 to 0.23 on the different-extractor
pair and from 0.85 to 0.54 on the same-extractor pair). Second, federated learning
does not collapse and beats single-network models in every setting we test, but
its quality is governed mainly by feature richness rather than by how close the
domains are: restricting the same-extractor pair to the same nine features lowers
MCC from 0.85 to 0.54, below the 0.65 of the different-extractor pair. Third, we
explain why adversarial training helps one domain but breaks another: it shifts
the global decision boundary in one uniform direction that cannot serve
heterogeneous clients at once. Cloud cost is small (about 300 KB and 16 seconds
per round). All numbers come from real executions; the protocol is released for
reproducibility.

Keywords: federated learning, network intrusion detection, cross-dataset generalization,
non-IID data, adversarial robustness, evaluation protocol, cloud deployment,
reproducibility

1. INTRODUCTION
================================================================================

Machine-learning based network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) are a core
component of cyber defense, yet accurate models demand large and representative
traffic data that organizations are rarely allowed to share for privacy and
compliance reasons. Federated learning (FL) offers a way out: each client trains
on its own data and exchanges only model updates, so raw traffic never leaves its
owner [1]. FL for NIDS has grown quickly and has been summarized by several
surveys and taxonomies [1], [2], [3], [4].

Despite this progress, FL-NIDS performance claims are hard to compare across
papers because each study uses different datasets, models, aggregation strategies,
and evaluation protocols, so reported numbers are not equivalent. Three challenges
recur. First, data heterogeneity (non-IID): FedAvg, the default aggregator, is
known to degrade when client distributions diverge, and is reported to fail in
cross-dataset scenarios [4], [5]. Second, adversarial and Byzantine vulnerability:
detection models can be fooled by small input perturbations (evasion), and
defenses such as adversarial training carry an unresolved robustness-accuracy
trade-off [6], [7], [8]. Third, scarce evidence of scalable public-cloud
deployment: most validations run on small edge testbeds or simulations, so real
communication and latency costs are poorly documented.

This paper asks a focused question: when does cross-dataset FL-NIDS actually work,
and why does it sometimes appear not to? We answer it with two dataset pairs. In
the near-domain pair both clients are extracted with the same tool (CICFlowMeter),
so the reliable feature intersection is large (76 features); the clients are
CIC-IDS2017 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018. In the far-domain pair the clients use different
extractors (CICFlowMeter vs Argus/Bro), so only nine features align; the clients
are CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15. Because the two pairs differ both in domain and
in the number of aligned features, we add a feature-controlled ablation that
restricts the near-domain pair to the same nine features. Together with a
leakage-free evaluation protocol, this separates "FL is hard across datasets" from
"our evaluation was inconsistent" and from "the shared feature space was poor." We
stress that this paper does not propose a feature-mapping method; feature
alignment here is plain column-name intersection, treated as experimental setup,
common in cross-dataset work [5].

Our contributions are: (1) a leakage-free evaluation protocol in which each
client's test set is normalized with that client's own training statistics,
matching how the federated model is trained, and a demonstration that an
inconsistent pooled evaluation severely and misleadingly deflates the measured
performance; (2) a feature-controlled comparison of two dataset pairs showing that
cross-dataset FL does not collapse and beats single-network models in every
setting, and that its quality tracks feature richness rather than domain
proximity; (3) an explanation of a previously open finding, namely why adversarial
training helps one domain but breaks another; and (4) a uniform, reproducible AWS
testbed with measured cloud cost, released as a baseline. The remainder of the
paper covers related work (Section 2), the problem setup and leakage-free protocol
(Section 3), results (Section 4), discussion and threats (Section 5), and
conclusions.

2. RELATED WORK
================================================================================

We organize recent FL-NIDS literature into four themes.

Architectures and aggregation. The dominant architecture is horizontal cross-silo,
in which organizations jointly train without sharing raw data [1], [11], with
FedAvg as the baseline aggregator [1]. Advanced variants include Entente [11]
(adaptive contribution scaling and graph sketching), FedAGRU [12] (attention-based
aggregation), FedIn-NID [13] (dynamic global aggregation for IIoT), FLAD [14]
(adaptive FedAvg for DDoS detection), and resource-efficient FL [15]. Most are
evaluated within one network and rarely cross-dataset.

Heterogeneity and non-IID data. Lavaur et al. [5] show FL-NIDS degrading in
cross-dataset scenarios spanning UNSW-NB15, Bot-IoT, ToN_IoT, and CSE-CIC-IDS2018.
Remedies include personalization and clustering (Sentinel [16]; FedDB [17]),
feature or behavior alignment [18], [11], hierarchical designs [19], and explicit
IID-versus-non-IID studies [20].

Adversarial robustness and Byzantine/poisoning. Adversarial training is the most
proposed evasion defense: Ennaji et al. [21] and SFAT [6] target robustness under
heterogeneity, and HADA [22] reports accuracy under FGSM/PGD. Byzantine defenses
reject malicious updates (FedSecIoT [23], Saha et al. [7], FORT-IDS [24]); GADoT
[25] uses a GAN for adversarial examples. Benchmarks and reviews survey this
landscape [26], [8], [27]. Integrating adversarial evaluation with a cross-dataset
setting remains rare.

Few-shot, meta-learning, and cross-domain transfer. Ceviz et al. [10] apply
few-shot FL; FeCoGraph [28] uses graph contrastive learning; Hu et al. [9] design
federated meta-learning; FedPKT [29] uses prototypical knowledge transfer; and an
ensemble of CNN, LSTM, and Transformer [30] reports high accuracy at high cost.
Combining alignment with truly cross-dataset evaluation is still uncommon.

Table 1 compares representative frameworks. The performance column is not
comparable across rows because each work uses different metrics and datasets,
which is exactly what motivates our controlled study.

3. PROBLEM SETUP AND LEAKAGE-FREE PROTOCOL
================================================================================

3.1 Dataset Pairs and Feature Alignment
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

We study two configurations. In the far-domain configuration the clients are
CSE-CIC-IDS2018 (CIC, CICFlowMeter, about 80 features) and UNSW-NB15 (UNSW,
Argus/Bro, 42 features); because the extractors differ, only nine features have a
one-to-one counterpart in both schemas (flow duration, forward/backward packet
counts and volumes, mean packet sizes, and load), so the shared space is nine
features (Fig. 1). In the near-domain configuration both clients are
CICFlowMeter-based (CIC-IDS2017 and CSE-CIC-IDS2018), so the reliable name
intersection is 76 features. The number of shared features is therefore a
consequence of the extractor intersection, not a design choice; such manual name
alignment is common in cross-dataset work [5] and we treat it as setup, not a
contribution. The two pairs therefore differ in more than one respect (extractor,
number of aligned features, and dataset difficulty), which is why Section 4 adds a
feature-controlled ablation on the near-domain pair.

[FIGURE 1. Far-domain feature alignment (9-feature intersection).]
(file: figure-fed/fig_feature_alignment.png)

3.2 Federated Architecture
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Our federation follows a horizontal cross-silo pattern with one aggregator and two
clients, each holding one dataset (Fig. 2). Each client trains on its own data and
returns only weight updates; feature normalization (z-score) is computed locally
per client, so no data statistics leak between clients. Each round the aggregator
sends the global model, each client trains locally, and the aggregator combines
updates with FedAvg (weighted by data size) for 100 rounds. In the far-domain runs
the three nodes are three t3.medium Amazon EC2 instances exchanging weights via
Amazon S3; the near-domain and non-IID sweeps are run as controlled simulations
with identical FedAvg, z-score, and model code. Run on the far-domain pair with
the same seeds, the simulation reproduces the EC2 result exactly (union MCC 0.647
± 0.020 in both), so the two pipelines are functionally equivalent.

[FIGURE 2. Federated learning architecture.]
(file: figure-fed/fig_fl_architecture.png)

3.3 Model, Metrics, and Claims
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

All clients use an identical lightweight multilayer perceptron (d→128→64→1 with
ReLU hidden units, sigmoid output, binary cross-entropy loss), trained with
mini-batch SGD (batch 256, learning rate 0.003) for five local epochs per round
over 100 rounds; d is 9 (far) or 76 (near). This shallow MLP is a deliberate
controlled backbone rather than a performance-maximizing choice. A small model
keeps the per-round payload tiny (about 76.8 KB per client), which is essential
for a cross-silo deployment and is itself the object of the R4 cost measurement;
it also isolates the effects we study—aggregation (FedAvg vs FedProx), non-IID
degree, cross-domain transfer, and the adversarial decision-score shift—from the
confounding training dynamics of large, high-capacity models, such as
latent-representation overfitting, whose behavior under federated aggregation is
harder to attribute. Because the architecture is held fixed and identical across
all clients and scenarios, any measured difference is attributable to the factor
under test rather than to model capacity. The primary metric is the Matthews
correlation coefficient (MCC), reliable under class imbalance; we also report
recall and attack success rate (ASR), the share of attacks detected before a
perturbation that evade detection afterwards. We verify six claims: R1 FedAvg vs
FedProx; R2 non-IID impact (Dirichlet α sweep); R3 cross-dataset feasibility; R4
cloud cost; R5 evasion (FGSM, PGD, JSMA, evaluated in feature space as an
upper-bound vulnerability analysis rather than packet-level wire transmission);
and R6 adversarial training as a defense. We use five seeds {13,42,101,202,303}
for R3/R5/R6 and the near-domain runs, and three seeds for R1/R2, reporting mean
and 95% confidence interval. The data partition is fixed, so variation reflects
training randomness only.

3.4 Leakage-Free Evaluation Protocol
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

A central methodological point is how test data is normalized. In the EC2
federated runs, z-score statistics are fit per client on its own training split
and never shared; to be consistent, each client's test set is normalized with its
owner's training statistics, and results are reported per client and on the union
of both test sets. This is the leakage-free protocol. For contrast we also report
a pooled evaluation, where all test data is normalized with statistics pooled
across clients; this is internally inconsistent with how the federated model was
trained and does not preserve statistic privacy. The centralized baseline is
reported with both pooled and per-domain (local) normalization, to separate the
effect of normalization from the effect of federation. As we show next, failing to
state the evaluation protocol can make the same federated model look like a
failure.

4. RESULTS
================================================================================

4.1 Far-Domain Verification (R1–R6)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Table 2 summarizes the six claims in the far-domain configuration.

[TABLE 2. Far-domain verification of six FL-NIDS claims.]
Claim	Our lab result	Holds?
R1 FedProx>FedAvg	0.572 vs. 0.571, n.s.	No
R2 non-IID harms FL	CIC drops; UNSW flat	Domain dep.
R3 cross-dataset hard	fed. 0.65 vs. cen. 0.62	No
R4 comm. cost	300 KB, 16 s / round	—
R5 evasion	ASR 0.91 at budget 0.1	Yes
R6 adv. training	CIC up; UNSW breaks	Domain dep.

R1 (FedProx vs FedAvg): does not hold. FedAvg and FedProx are statistically
indistinguishable (MCC 0.572 vs 0.571), both converging in 7–11 rounds.

R2 (non-IID impact): domain dependent. On Client-CIC, MCC is stable at high α and
falls to 0.387 at α=0.1 with a large standard deviation; on Client-UNSW it stays
flat or rises. The CIC mean is driven by a single run: at α=0.1 two of three seeds
score 0.57–0.59 and one collapses to MCC 0 (a degenerate model that predicts a
single class). A follow-up that swapped the attack ratios (CIC raised to 55%, UNSW
lowered to 17%, same protocol, three seeds) did not reproduce the asymmetry as a
difference in mean decline: the drop from α=100 to α=0.1 was 0.07 for CIC at 55%
and 0.02 for UNSW at 17%. Collapsed runs (MCC below 0.1) occurred only at the low
attack ratio (3 of 24 runs versus 0 of 24; Fisher p=0.23), which is suggestive but
not significant with three seeds. We therefore report the CIC–UNSW asymmetry as an
observation of occasional collapse under extreme non-IID, and we do not establish
the attack ratio as its cause.

R3 (cross-dataset FL is hard): does not hold under a consistent protocol. Under
the leakage-free protocol the federated model reaches a union-test MCC of 0.647 ±
0.020 (five seeds; CIC 0.533, UNSW 0.610). A centralized model reaches 0.565 ±
0.028 (pooled norm) and 0.616 ± 0.012 (per-domain norm), so at matched
normalization the federated model is slightly but consistently better (difference
+0.031, Welch p=0.009). Single-network (local-only) models fail to transfer:
trained on CIC they reach 0.226 on the union set, trained on UNSW only 0.010.
Crucially, the same federated model evaluated with pooled statistics inconsistent
with its training falls to 0.229 ± 0.228, erratic across seeds—an evaluation
artifact, not a model failure (Fig. 3).

R4 (cloud cost): measured. Each client upload is 76.8 KB, so aggregator traffic is
about 300 KB per round; 100 rounds take about 27 minutes (about 16 seconds per
round including S3 sync).

R5 (evasion): holds. We state the threat model explicitly: the attack operates in
the tabular flow-feature space under a white-box assumption, and perturbations are
clipped per feature to its 1st–99th training percentile but are not constrained to
preserve cross-feature protocol consistency (for example, changing flow duration
without re-deriving the corresponding byte and packet counts). Our ASR is
therefore a measure of feature-space vulnerability as a worst-case upper bound,
not of packet-level deliverability; we do not claim the perturbed flows could be
transmitted on the wire. This framing is intentional: it isolates how fragile the
learned decision surface is, independent of the engineering effort an attacker
would need to realize such a flow. On a healthy base (recall 0.61, MCC 0.647), at
ε=0.1 ASR reaches 0.89 (FGSM) and 0.91 (PGD); for ε≥0.2 ASR is at least 0.98 and
MCC turns negative. JSMA, changing only two features, is weaker (ASR 0.37–0.91).

R6 (adversarial training): domain dependent. On CIC it helps (MCC under PGD
0.149→0.500, ASR 0.74→0.13), but on UNSW the clean MCC drops from 0.610 to 0.170
and recall from 0.70 to 0.10. We explain this in Section 5.

(file: figure-fed/fig_results.png)

4.3 Feature-Controlled Ablation
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The near-domain pair reaches a higher MCC than the far-domain pair, but the two
pairs differ in both domain and feature count (76 vs 9). To separate the two
factors we rerun the near-domain federation using only the nine features that
define the far-domain space (the same column names after alias matching); every
other setting is identical: model, FedAvg, 100 rounds, five seeds, and the
leakage-free protocol. Table 5 reports the result.

[TABLE 5. Feature-controlled ablation: union-test MCC (mean ± 95% CI, five seeds). Local-only: the two single-client models.]
Pair	Feat.	Federated	Central.	Local-only
Near (2017+2018)	76	0.851 ± 0.010	0.828 ± 0.017	0.433 / 0.598
Near (2017+2018)	9	0.537 ± 0.022	0.435 ± 0.012	0.444 / 0.430
Far (2018+UNSW)	9	0.647 ± 0.020	0.565 ± 0.028	0.226 / 0.010
Far, UNSW at 17%	9	0.614 ± 0.002	–	–

Reducing the near-domain pair from 76 to 9 features lowers the federated union MCC
from 0.851 to 0.537 ± 0.022 (Welch p<10^-7 over five seeds). At the same nine
features the near-domain pair is below the far-domain pair (0.537 vs 0.647,
p<10^-5). Feature richness therefore explains the 0.65→0.85 gap, whereas domain
proximity, as measured by a shared extractor, does not. Federation keeps its
advantage at nine features: it exceeds centralized training (0.435 with pooled and
0.372 with local normalization) and both local-only models (0.444 and 0.430). The
pairs also differ in class balance: UNSW-NB15 is attack-majority (55.1%). To test
whether this explains the far-domain advantage, we reran the far-domain federation
in simulation with UNSW resampled to 17% attacks (train and test), close to the
near-domain pair (last row of Table 5). The union MCC fell only from 0.647 to
0.614 ± 0.002 (-0.033, p=0.01), which accounts for about 30% of the 0.11 gap to
the near-domain pair at nine features. The remaining 0.077 (p<10^-3) is not
explained by the attack ratio; we attribute it provisionally to differences in
separability between the datasets, which we did not test. Per client, the lower
UNSW ratio even raised CIC (0.533 to 0.610), so a partner's class balance affects
the other client.

5. DISCUSSION AND THREATS TO VALIDITY
================================================================================

5.1 What Governs Cross-Dataset FL Quality
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Across both dataset pairs and the feature-controlled ablation, four lessons are
robust. (i) Cross-dataset FL does not collapse: it matches or exceeds centralized
training wherever we measured both, and the same holds at nine features. (ii)
Single-network local-only models always fail to transfer, so federation gives a
real benefit in every setting. (iii) The conclusion is sensitive to the evaluation
protocol: the same federated model scores 0.65/0.85 under the leakage-free
protocol but only 0.23/0.54 under an inconsistent pooled evaluation (and 0.54/0.34
at nine features on the near-domain pair). The claim that cross-dataset FL is hard
[5] therefore cannot be assessed without stating the normalization protocol and a
matched baseline. (iv) Quality is governed mainly by feature richness rather than
by domain proximity: with the feature count held at nine, the shared-extractor
pair is not better than the different-extractor pair (Table 5), and moving that
pair from nine to 76 features raises the union MCC by 0.31.

We therefore do not claim that FL improves as domains get closer. Our data cannot
isolate a pure domain-proximity effect: equalizing the attack ratio closes only
about 30% of the remaining gap, and the pairs still differ in dataset difficulty.
A cleaner test would use pairs with the same extractor, the same feature set, and
matched difficulty, for example datasets re-extracted with one standardized flow
exporter; we leave this to future work.

5.2 Why Adversarial Training Breaks One Domain
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

R6 is domain dependent, and we traced the cause by comparing decision-score
distributions of the clean and adversarially trained models over five seeds (Fig.
5). Adversarial training shifts the global decision score uniformly toward
"attack," which helps CIC but harms UNSW because the two domains start from
different points. On CIC the mean attack score rises from 0.534 to 0.751, pushing
attacks past the 0.5 threshold and raising recall from 0.48 to 0.91. On UNSW the
mean attack score instead falls from 0.687 to 0.325, crossing the threshold the
other way, so recall collapses from 0.70 to 0.10 and the fraction of flows flagged
as attacks drops from 0.49 to 0.07. Because FGSM examples from both clients are
aggregated into one global model via FedAvg while each client uses its own local
z-score, the direction that improves CIC opposes the one UNSW needs; the global
model cannot satisfy both and favors CIC, whose attacks are harder to detect. The
degradation is thus not random instability (it is consistent across seeds) but a
cross-domain update-direction conflict under a static, uniform defense—direct
evidence that domain-aware adversarial defenses are needed in heterogeneous FL.

[FIGURE 5. Decision-score distributions (benign vs attack), clean vs adversarially trained, per domain. After adversarial training the UNSW attack scores shift below the 0.5 threshold and are no longer detected.]
(file: figure-fed/fig_unsw_degradation.png)

5.3 Threats to Validity
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

First, we measure whether the direction of a claim holds on a uniform testbed, not
an exact replication, so absolute differences from the original papers are
expected. Second, the scope is intentionally narrow: two datasets per
configuration and two clients, with three seeds for R1/R2 and five for the rest;
the confidence intervals capture training randomness only, since the partition is
fixed. The decision-score explanation of the UNSW degradation is based on one
dataset pair and should be tested on more pairs. Third, normalization and the
evaluation protocol strongly affect results, and the EC2 (per-client z-score) and
simulation (shared global z-score for R1/R2) figures should not be compared
directly. Fourth, the adversarial experiments act on the tabular flow-feature
space, not raw packets, and this is a deliberate scoping choice rather than an
oversight: perturbations are clipped to the 1st–99th percentile of each feature
but do not enforce cross-feature protocol or functional constraints (for instance,
byte, packet, and duration relationships), so a perturbed flow need not be
physically realizable on a real network. We therefore interpret the results as a
feature-space, worst-case robustness baseline under a white-box threat model,
deliberately separate from packet-level deliverability; demonstrating that such
perturbations can be realized as sendable traffic is a distinct problem we do not
address. Finally, ASR is computed only over attacks detected before the
perturbation and is not comparable across models with different recall. Fifth, we
deliberately fix a single shallow MLP as a controlled backbone (Section 3), so our
findings characterize federated behavior at a fixed, low model capacity; whether
the cross-domain performance gap and the adversarial decision-score shift persist
under deeper architectures (for example 1D-CNN or transformer encoders) is not
established here and is left to future work, which would separate model-capacity
limits from genuinely federated effects.

5.4 Research Directions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The most pressing directions are standardized, leakage-free normalization and
evaluation protocols for cross-dataset FL; domain-aware evasion defenses instead
of static adversarial training; verification of the reported phenomena under
deeper architectures (1D-CNN, transformer) to test whether they are
capacity-independent; semantic-aware feature alignment (which we pursue
separately); and validation at a client and traffic scale closer to production,
including IoT traffic domains.

6. CONCLUSION
================================================================================

We studied when cross-dataset federated learning works for network intrusion
detection on two dataset pairs, under a leakage-free evaluation protocol and with
a feature-controlled ablation. Cross-dataset FL does not collapse and consistently
beats single-network models, which fail to transfer. Its quality is governed
mainly by feature richness rather than domain proximity: the same-extractor pair
reaches a union MCC of 0.85 with 76 features but only 0.54 with the nine features
of the different-extractor pair, which reaches 0.65. The apparent collapse
reported for cross-dataset FL can be an evaluation artifact: the same federated
model falls to 0.23–0.54 when normalized inconsistently with its training. A
healthy model remains highly vulnerable to evasion (ASR 0.91 at budget 0.1), and
adversarial training is domain dependent because it shifts the global decision
boundary in a single direction that cannot serve heterogeneous clients at
once—helping one domain while breaking another. Cloud cost is small (about 300 KB
and 16 seconds per round). The main contribution is evidence and protocol rather
than a new method: a reproducible, reasonably priced baseline that shows which
FL-NIDS claims are robust, which are domain dependent, and which are sensitive to
the evaluation protocol. All protocols, scripts, and cloud configurations are
released so others can reproduce and build on them.
