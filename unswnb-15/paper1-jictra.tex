\begin{abstract}
\noindent Machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) work well on their
training network but often fail on a different one. One cause is easy to overlook:
different flow-export tools compute nominally identical features differently, so a model
trained on one tool can misread another tool's output. We call this a
\emph{feature-extractor mismatch}. To handle it, we build a \emph{Semantic Feature
Mapping} (SFM): a statistics-based audit that checks whether a feature from one tool
truly matches a feature from another in meaning and in scale. Mismatched pairs, such as
the TCP-window features, are rejected, leaving a nine-feature space shared across tools.
Using two public datasets (CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15) with a lightweight XGBoost
detector, a single-source model that is accurate within its own network collapses across
networks; adding only 1\% of labelled target data restores most of the lost accuracy. On
a small, controlled AWS cloud testbed the false-alarm rate stays low, and few-shot
calibration recovers detection provided the calibration data reflect the test traffic.
The cross-network gap is thus largely a distribution-shift problem that can be closed
cheaply once the feature space is made comparable: the best cross-network MCC rises from
near zero to 0.659 and 0.900 with 1\% target labels.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} network intrusion detection; cross-network
generalization; semantic feature mapping; feature-extractor mismatch; cloud testbed
validation; few-shot calibration; edge deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cyberattacks impose major and growing economic losses \cite{cremer}, making
ML-based NIDS a critical line of defense \cite{khraisat,buczak}. Such NIDS routinely
report over 99\% accuracy on public benchmarks, yet this rarely materializes as a
working detector on a different, previously unseen network. The gap stems from a
seldom-tested assumption: that training and operational traffic share the same
distribution. Prior cross-dataset studies \cite{cantone,dhooge,verkerken} confirm
the collapse---average MCC falling from 0.946 within-dataset to 0.294 cross-dataset
\cite{cantone}---but they generally stop at observing it, and they evaluate only
dataset-to-dataset, without testing on a live network testbed.

Two deployment-facing gaps run through the studies we surveyed
(Table~\ref{tab:related}). They are rarely addressed together.

The first is \textbf{feature-extractor mismatch}. Different flow exporters compute the
same nominal feature in different ways. A unified schema that matches features by name
therefore admits pairs that are not comparable. We detect such pairs with an exploratory,
statistics-based scale-equivalence audit and reject them. Where exact equivalence is not
possible across schemas, we keep the pair but document the directional or unit proxy
openly.

The second gap is the \textbf{absence of validation on a live network}. Cross-dataset
papers measure drops from one dataset to another. The surveyed studies do not deploy the
mapped model on a live cloud testbed, so they never observe its false-alarm behaviour or
the conditions under which calibration helps.

\paragraph{Core novelty.} What is new here is the \emph{combination}. We carry a
statistically audited cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping all the way to a controlled
live cloud testbed on AWS. There we measure the false-alarm rate under benign traffic,
quantify a three-domain distribution shift, and test when a small amount of few-shot
calibration restores detection. Each piece has precedent on its own. Unified-feature-set
work \cite{sarhan} standardises schemas but audits no pair for scale-equivalence and runs
on no live testbed. Cross-dataset studies \cite{cantone,dhooge,verkerken} document the
collapse but offer neither live validation nor a low-cost repair. We join the two lines
of work.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We make four contributions. First, a cross-tool SFM whose
per-pair, unit-corrected audit separates seven exact semantic equivalents, two documented
rate proxies with bounded definitional differences (\texttt{src\_load} and
\texttt{dst\_load}), and the incompatible TCP-window features it discards. Second,
quantitative evidence of cross-network collapse, with a controlled diagnosis that
separates feature insufficiency from distribution shift. Third, a low-cost repair using
1\% target labels, reported with 5-seed confidence intervals and an indicative
single-seed comparison against cross-dataset mixup. Fourth, end-to-end validation on the
live AWS testbed: a benign false-alarm rate, a quantified three-domain shift, and a
two-protocol few-shot evaluation (stratified flow-level versus unseen-attack
episode-level) showing that the value of calibration depends on how representative the
target labels are.

\paragraph{Research questions.} RQ1: can compatible flow features (exact semantic
equivalents and documented rate proxies) from heterogeneous tools be reliably audited and
separated from extractor mismatches? RQ2: how far does a single-source NIDS generalize
after SFM alignment? RQ3: what distinguishes representation insufficiency from
distribution shift? RQ4: how much target labeling recovers performance? RQ5: under what
conditions does the recipe transfer to a live AWS cloud testbed?

\paragraph{Reproducibility.} All reported values come from executed experiments; no
illustrative values are presented as results, and negative/non-monotone outcomes are
reported as observed.

\section{Related Work}
ML-based NIDS span tree ensembles and deep models (e.g., deep and transformer-based
detectors \cite{shone,wu}, and XGBoost variants on UNSW-NB15 \cite{husain}), most
reporting strong in-domain results that do not guarantee cross-network robustness.

\paragraph{Cross-dataset generalization.} D'Hooge et al.\ \cite{dhooge} showed
supervised NIDS fail on unseen networks; Verkerken et al.\ \cite{verkerken} reported
about 30\% AUROC drop with unsupervised models; Cantone et al.\ \cite{cantone}
documented MCC falling from 0.946 (within) to 0.294 (cross) over four datasets.
These works confirm the collapse but stop at observing it, without separating
feature insufficiency from distribution shift and without a low-cost recovery or
live-testbed validation.

\paragraph{Unified feature sets.} Sarhan et al.\ \cite{sarhan,sarhannf} proposed a
standardized NetFlow feature set to enable cross-dataset comparison. We go further:
each mapped pair is audited by a statistics-based scale-equivalence check on its
original scale, and spuriously named matches are rejected (the TCP-window case)---exactly
what a name-based schema admits silently.

\paragraph{Dataset integrity.} Reconstructions found over 20\% of CIC-IDS2017 flows
needed relabeling \cite{engelen}, CICFlowMeter feature errors \cite{rosay,lanvin},
and 6--7\% label-error rates on CIC/CSE-CIC datasets \cite{liu}. Catillo et al.\
\cite{catillo} questioned whether benchmark progress reflects real advances. Layeghy
et al.\ \cite{layeghy} showed synthetic benign traffic differs statistically (large
Wasserstein distance) from real networks---motivating both our cross-tool mapping
and our live cloud testbed validation.

\paragraph{Domain adaptation.} CORAL \cite{coral} aligns covariance; few-shot methods
target label-limited NIDS. Deep domain-invariant NIDS learns adversarially invariant
features but needs a heavy deep stack and ample target data---impractical for edge.
We keep a fixed lightweight XGBoost \cite{xgboost} backbone and recover performance
with cheap per-deployment calibration, a different edge-oriented operating point. A
head-to-head against deep DA needs a different stack and is left as future work
(scope stated explicitly, not an unmeasured superiority claim).

\paragraph{Positioning against closest work.} We do not claim that any single component
is new. We claim the \emph{combination}, and we state what we add to the nearest studies
(Table~\ref{tab:related}). The comparison is against the studies we surveyed, not an
absolute first.

\section{Data and Semantic Feature Mapping}
\paragraph{Datasets (deliberately cross-source).} CSE-CIC-IDS2018 \cite{cicids}
(CICFlowMeter, 68 clean features) and UNSW-NB15 \cite{unsw,unsweval} (Argus and
Bro/Zeek, 42 features; 175{,}341 train and 82{,}332 test records). The differing
extraction tools are a prerequisite for realistically testing feature-extractor
mismatch, not a weakness. All attack types are collapsed to a single
\texttt{attack} class (controlled binary setting).

\paragraph{SFM construction: an exploratory, statistics-based equivalence audit.} SFM
maps functionally compatible features across flow exporters even when column names differ
(e.g., \texttt{Flow Duration}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dur}). This is an \emph{exploratory,
statistics-based audit of semantic and scale compatibility}, not a formal hypothesis test
or formal proof of semantic equivalence; it screens candidate pairs by
order-of-magnitude agreement of robust summary statistics after accounting for physical
units. Steps: (i) verify feature semantics, directional scope, and units from each tool's
official documentation; (ii) return CIC features to original scale via
Eq.~\eqref{eq:unscale} (as the CIC release is z-scored); (iii) compute the raw
between-tool ratios of the median and 99th percentile (p99),
$r_{\mathrm{med}}=\mathrm{med}_{\mathrm{CIC}}/\mathrm{med}_{\mathrm{UNSW}}$ and
$r_{\mathrm{p99}}=\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{CIC}}/\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{UNSW}}$, and for
pairs sharing a documented physical unit conversion factor $c$ (from CIC units to UNSW
units: $c = 10^{-6}\text{ s}/\mu\text{s}$ for duration, $c = 10^{-3}\text{ ms}/\mu\text{s}$
for IAT, $c = 8\text{ bit/byte}$ for byte-vs-bit rate, and $c = 1$ when units already
match), compute the unit-corrected ratios $r'_{\mathrm{med}} = c \cdot r_{\mathrm{med}}$
and $r'_{\mathrm{p99}} = c \cdot r_{\mathrm{p99}}$; (iv) classify each pair by the
two-stage rule in Eq.~\eqref{eq:verdict}. The thresholds are decision-rule heuristics
applied uniformly to all pairs and reported in full for reproducibility. Both
\emph{aligned} and \emph{scale-mismatch} pairs are \emph{retained} (remaining
distributional scale differences are resolved by per-dataset z-score), alongside
documented rate proxies where schemas lack exact directional/unit counterparts;
incompatible \emph{different-feature} pairs are \emph{rejected}.

\paragraph{Basis for selecting the two rate proxies (\texttt{src\_load} and
\texttt{dst\_load}) and impact evaluation.} We do \emph{not} claim that all nine features
in Model~A possess exact semantic equivalence across CSE-CIC-IDS2018, UNSW-NB15, and the
AWS NFStream extractor. Specifically, Model~A comprises \emph{seven exact semantic
equivalents} (\texttt{dur}, \texttt{spkts}, \texttt{dpkts}, \texttt{sbytes},
\texttt{dbytes}, \texttt{smean}, and \texttt{dmean}, which measure the exact same flow
quantities and directions across all three domains) plus \emph{two documented rate
proxies} that bridge schema differences between flow exporters:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Rationale and definitional scope of the two rate proxies:}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Forward/total byte-rate proxy (\texttt{src\_load}:
    \texttt{Flow Byts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{sload}):} In UNSW-NB15 (\texttt{sload},
    bits/s) and in our AWS NFStream extractor ($\texttt{src2dst\_bytes} / \texttt{dur\_s}$,
    bytes/s), \texttt{src\_load} measures unidirectional \emph{forward} byte rate
    (differing only by the unit factor $c = 8$ bits/byte). However, the 68-feature
    CSE-CIC-IDS2018 release omits a separate forward byte-rate column
    (\texttt{Fwd Byts/s}) and exports only total bidirectional byte rate
    ($\texttt{Flow Byts/s} = (\texttt{TotLen Fwd Pkts} + \texttt{TotLen Bwd Pkts}) /
    \texttt{Flow Duration}$ in bytes/s). Because manually synthesizing
    $\texttt{TotLen Fwd Pkts} / \texttt{Flow Duration}$ introduces division-by-zero
    instability on zero-duration flows, \texttt{Flow Byts/s} is mapped to \texttt{sload}
    with unit factor $c = 8$ bits/byte. While the two quantities coincide on
    unidirectional floods (e.g., SYN/UDP floods where backward bytes are zero),
    \texttt{Flow Byts/s} includes backward bytes on bidirectional flows. We therefore
    classify \texttt{Flow Byts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{sload} as a directional-scope
    rate proxy (\emph{dir-proxy}$^{\ast}$ in Table~\ref{tab:sfm}) rather than an
    identical feature across all three domains, and state this as an explicit
    methodological limitation.
    \item \emph{Backward rate proxy (\texttt{dst\_load}:
    \texttt{Bwd Pkts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dload}):} Conversely, CICFlowMeter and
    our AWS extractor measure backward rate in packets/s (\texttt{Bwd Pkts/s} and
    $\texttt{dst2src\_packets} / \texttt{dur\_s}$) and provide no native backward
    byte-rate column, whereas Argus/UNSW-NB15 exports backward rate only in bits/s
    (\texttt{dload}) and provides no backward packet-rate column. Because
    backward-direction rate is essential to separate asymmetric request--response traffic
    from unidirectional floods, \texttt{Bwd Pkts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dload} is
    retained as a unit-coupling rate proxy (\emph{rate-proxy}$^{\sharp}$ in
    Table~\ref{tab:sfm}), where packets/s and bits/s are coupled by per-flow backward
    packet size rather than a constant factor $c$.
  \end{itemize}
  \item \emph{Empirical evaluation of proxy impact (non-causal subset sensitivity):}
  Three experimental checks quantify the effect of retaining these two proxies. First, in
  joint-model feature importance and subset ablation ($k \in \{3, 5, 7, 9\}$ top-ranked
  features, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), \texttt{dst\_load} ranks second in discriminative
  importance behind \texttt{fwd\_bytes} (entering at $k=3$) while \texttt{src\_load}
  ranks eighth (entering at $k=9$); together with the exact-equivalence features,
  expanding from $k=3$ to $k=9$ lifts in-domain UNSW MCC from $0.702$ to $0.744$ and
  joint UNSW MCC from $0.646$ to $0.731$. Second, the feature-count ablation
  (Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) shows that cross-network transfer performance is sensitive
  to feature-subset composition: the minimal subset $k=3$ (\texttt{fwd\_bytes,
  dst\_load, duration}) yields partial positive transfer on CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW
  ($+0.377$; UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC $+0.065$) alongside lower in-domain and joint UNSW MCC
  ($0.702$ and $0.646$), whereas expanding the subset to $k \in \{5, 7, 9\}$ improves
  in-domain UNSW MCC ($0.728$ to $0.744$) and joint UNSW MCC ($0.719$ to $0.731$) but
  results in near-zero cross-network MCC ($-0.057$ to $-0.009$). While this
  non-monotonic pattern does not by itself isolate a single causal mechanism, the fact
  that \texttt{dst\_load} is already present at $k=3$ (where CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW reaches
  $+0.377$) and that transfer is already near zero at $k=5$ and $k=7$ (before
  \texttt{src\_load} is added at $k=9$) indicates that cross-network collapse at
  $k \in \{5, 7, 9\}$ is not solely induced by either rate proxy. Third, because
  definitional and packet-size differences amplify inter-domain spread on rate features,
  \texttt{Flow Byts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{sload} and
  \texttt{Bwd Pkts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dload} exhibit the two largest UNSW
  variances in joint z-score space ($9.00$ and $9.50$ vs $0.00$ and $0.01$ in CIC,
  Table~\ref{tab:dispersion}), which are subsequently mitigated once 1\% target labels
  recalibrate the scaler and tree thresholds (lifting MCC to $0.659$ and $0.900$).
\end{enumerate}

Nine retained features (7 exact semantic equivalents $+$ 2 documented rate proxies) form
Model~A; two inter-arrival-time pairs form optional Model~B;
two TCP-window pairs are discarded as a feature-extractor mismatch (continuous in
CICFlowMeter, effectively binary in Argus/Bro).

\begin{equation}
  x_{\mathrm{orig}} = x_{\mathrm{scaled}} \cdot \sigma + \mu
  \label{eq:unscale}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
  \text{verdict} =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{aligned} & \text{constant } c \text{ exists, } \max(|\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{med}}|, |\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{p99}}|) \le 1,\\
    \text{scale-mismatch} & \text{constant } c \text{ exists, } \max(|\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{med}}|, |\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{p99}}|) > 1,\\
    \text{rate-proxy} & \text{rate proxy (directional-scope difference or no constant } c\text{)},\\
    \text{different-feature} & \text{incompatible representation/semantics (rejected).}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:verdict}
\end{equation}

\noindent The audit is \emph{two-stage}: Stage~1 checks whether both tools measure the
same physical quantity up to a constant unit factor $c$ (or a documented rate proxy), and
Stage~2 grades exact-unit pairs into \emph{aligned} vs \emph{scale-mismatch} using the
unit-corrected ratios $r'_{\mathrm{med}}$ and $r'_{\mathrm{p99}}$ (evaluated on non-zero
quantiles). Under Eq.~\eqref{eq:verdict}, a pair is labeled \emph{aligned} only if
\emph{both} $r'_{\mathrm{med}}$ and $r'_{\mathrm{p99}}$ fall within $[0.1, 10]$ (i.e.,
$\max(|\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{med}}|, |\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{p99}}|) \le 1$); if either
quantile ratio exceeds one order of magnitude (for instance,
\texttt{Tot Bwd Pkts}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dpkts} has $r'_{\mathrm{med}} = 0.50$ within
bounds, but $r'_{\mathrm{p99}} = 0.09 < 0.1$), the pair is consistently labeled
\emph{scale-mismatch}. Table~\ref{tab:sfm} reports \emph{both} the raw ratios
($r_{\mathrm{med}}, r_{\mathrm{p99}}$) and the unit-corrected ratios
($r'_{\mathrm{med}}, r'_{\mathrm{p99}}$) recomputed from the unrounded quantiles. Thus,
\texttt{Flow Duration}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dur} (raw
$r_{\mathrm{med}} = 1.3{\times}10^{7}$, $r_{\mathrm{p99}} = 2.6{\times}10^{6}$) reduces
after $\mu\text{s}\rightarrow\text{s}$ conversion ($c = 10^{-6}$) to
$r'_{\mathrm{med}} = 13.1$ and $r'_{\mathrm{p99}} = 2.60$, and
\texttt{Fwd IAT Mean}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{sinpkt} (raw
$r_{\mathrm{med}} = 1.3{\times}10^{4}$, $r_{\mathrm{p99}} = 1.4{\times}10^{3}$) reduces
after $\mu\text{s}\rightarrow\text{ms}$ conversion ($c = 10^{-3}$) to
$r'_{\mathrm{med}} = 12.7$ and $r'_{\mathrm{p99}} = 1.44$, whereas the TCP-window pairs
(continuous $0$--$65535$ vs binary $\{0,255\}$) have no valid unit mapping and are
rejected.

\paragraph{Key finding (TCP-window mismatch).} UNSW \texttt{swin}/\texttt{dwin} are
practically binary ($\{0,255\}$) while CIC \texttt{Init Fwd/Bwd Win Byts} are
continuous ($0$--$65535$). Despite both being named ``TCP window,'' the data are not
comparable, so we discard the pair as a feature-extractor mismatch. Final Model~A
(9 features: 7 exact semantic equivalents $+$ 2 documented rate proxies):
\texttt{dur, spkts, dpkts, sbytes, dbytes, smean, dmean, sload, dload}; z-score is fit on

\section{Cross-Network Baseline and Diagnosis}
\paragraph{Collapse (RQ2).} A single XGBoost excels in-domain (MCC 0.91 on CIC, 0.74
on UNSW) but collapses cross-network (MCC $\approx 0$, Figure~\ref{fig:gap})---consistent
with the literature but here measured on the SFM-aligned feature space, ruling out a
trivial schema mismatch as the cause. We report MCC as the primary metric
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcc}) because it is robust under heavy class imbalance.

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}
  {\sqrt{(TP{+}FP)(TP{+}FN)(TN{+}FP)(TN{+}FN)}}
  \label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Diagnosis: shift, not (only) insufficiency (RQ3).} Joint training on the
nine SFM features performs well on both domains (5-seed mean MCC $0.913$ on CIC, SD
$0.001$, CI$_{95\%}$ $[0.912, 0.914]$; $0.730$ on UNSW, SD $0.004$, CI$_{95\%}$
$[0.726, 0.735]$), showing the shared features carry sufficient discriminative
information---so feature insufficiency is not the sole explanation
(Figure~\ref{fig:align}). By contrast, unsupervised covariance alignment via CORAL
\cite{coral} fails to recover cross-network detection reliably across the five seeds
(CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW mean MCC $-0.118$, SD $0.147$, CI$_{95\%}$ $[-0.300, 0.064]$;
UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC mean MCC $-0.032$, SD $0.156$, CI$_{95\%}$ $[-0.225, 0.162]$),
because matching first- and second-order moments without target labels does not align
class-conditional decision boundaries under severe attack-composition shift. (We report
Table~\ref{tab:baseline} as single-run to isolate the Model~A-vs-B feature-set
comparison, and the 5-seed means with 3-decimal CIs in Table~\ref{tab:fewshot}; across
seeds, CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW spans zero while UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC is consistently
slightly negative, and both confirm near-zero non-functional cross-network detection.) A
domain classifier separates CIC from UNSW with approximately $0.990$ accuracy (Proxy
$\mathcal{A}$-distance $= 1.961$) and a large inter-domain 1-Wasserstein distance (mean
$W_1 = 0.317$ on unit-corrected shared z-space, Table~\ref{tab:rtshift}), directly
measuring the distribution shift as the major contributor. This is conceptually motivated
by the Ben-David bound on target-domain error (Eq.~\eqref{eq:bendavid}), where a larger domain divergence upper-bounds achievable
cross-domain performance. A high domain-classifier accuracy shows the two domains are
easily separable but does not by itself prove shift is the only cause; the controlled
experiments indicate that distribution shift is a \emph{major contributor} to
cross-network failure, although other dataset-specific factors (differing label
taxonomies, attack composition, dataset artifacts, and the two rate-proxy approximations)
may also play a role.

\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_T(h) \le \epsilon_S(h) + d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(S,T) + \lambda
  \label{eq:bendavid}
\end{equation}

\section{Low-Cost Recovery (RQ4)}
Minimal calibration recovers cross-network detection: using only 1\% of
target-network labels, or supervised cross-dataset \emph{mixup} that uses only target
train labels, restores cross-network MCC into the 0.65--0.91 range across five random
seeds (Table~\ref{tab:fewshot} and Figure~\ref{fig:fewshot}). Whereas unsupervised CORAL
($0\%$ target labels) remains near zero ($-0.118$ on CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW; $-0.032$ on
UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC), 1\% target labels lifts 5-seed mean MCC from $-0.074$ to $0.659$
(CI$_{95\%}$ $[0.64, 0.68]$) on CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW and from $-0.094$ to $0.900$
(CI$_{95\%}$ $[0.90, 0.90]$) on UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC, capturing $90.3\%$ and $98.6\%$ of
the respective joint-training references ($0.730$ and $0.913$). Increasing the target
budget to 5\% and 10\% yields diminishing additional gains ($0.689$ and $0.691$ on
CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW; $0.909$ and $0.911$ on UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC), while cross-dataset
mixup reaches $0.680$ and $0.798$. We rely directly on held-out target MCC (with 5-seed
confidence intervals) as the primary quantitative evidence of recovery, because computing
raw-feature Wasserstein shifts prior to explicit cross-extractor unit alignment is
dominated by unit-scale differences (e.g., $\mu\text{s}$ vs $\text{s}$ in duration);
unit-corrected shared-$z$-space Wasserstein distances are instead reported in Section~6
(Table~\ref{tab:rtshift}) to characterize the baseline three-domain shift.

An adaptation-direction asymmetry is observed and analyzed: CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW recovers
lower (about 0.66--0.69) than UNSW$\rightarrow$CIC (about 0.90), explained by greater
domain dispersion in UNSW (mean SFM-feature variance 3.64 vs 0.68 for CIC, about
$5.3\times$, Table~\ref{tab:dispersion}), concentrated in rate/packet-size features. We
report this as an evidence-consistent interpretation, not a causal proof.

\section{Live Cloud Testbed Validation on AWS (RQ5)}
We evaluate the SFM-aligned detector on a controlled live AWS cloud testbed (two EC2
instances in \texttt{ap-southeast-1} inside an isolated private VPC, capturing raw
packets via \texttt{tcpdump} into NFStream to extract the 9 SFM features), a live-testbed
step omitted by the surveyed cross-dataset studies (Table~\ref{tab:related}). We frame
this experiment explicitly as a controlled live cloud testbed validation rather than a
broad multi-network production deployment: all traffic is generated between two instances
in a single VPC, and attack ground truth is assigned from the scheduled execution
timeline. During pipeline engineering, we aligned each of the 9 SFM features extracted
from NFStream to the CIC-trained deployment scaler. Two alignment points matter. First,
units: NFStream reports duration in milliseconds and raw directional byte/packet
counters, so we express duration in microseconds (to match CICFlowMeter) and compute the
rate features with a per-second denominator (guarding against division by zero), applied
once with no secondary rescaling downstream. Second, directional scope: seven features
(\texttt{duration}, \texttt{fwd\_pkts}, \texttt{bwd\_pkts}, \texttt{fwd\_bytes},
\texttt{bwd\_bytes}, \texttt{fwd\_mean}, \texttt{bwd\_mean}) are defined identically
across CIC, UNSW, and AWS, whereas the two rate proxies carry the bounded differences
documented in Section~3---notably, AWS \texttt{src\_load} measures forward byte-rate while
CICFlowMeter \texttt{Flow Byts/s} is bidirectional, so \texttt{src\_load} is not strictly
identical across all three domains (coinciding only on unidirectional floods). We treat
this as a methodological limitation when interpreting zero-shot and CIC+AWS transfer.
Across the two-phase testbed evaluation, we establish three findings:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Phase 1: Initial operational stability under benign testbed traffic.}
  Across three cumulative monitoring windows on the live target server (3~min, 1~h, and
  6~h; Table~\ref{tab:far}), false-alarm rate stays below 0.5\% without temporal drift
  ($0.00\%$ at 3~min over $203$ flows; $0.38\%$ at 1~h over $3{,}687$ flows; $0.42\%$ over
  $22{,}644$ flows at 6~h, Wilson CI$_{95\%}$ $[0.34\%, 0.51\%]$). While this 6-hour
  single-VPC testbed run does not represent the application diversity of multi-network
  production traffic and does not by itself prove the absence of all scaling or
  directional discrepancies across every feature, the observed benign-traffic FAR
  supports the effectiveness of the duration- and rate-unit alignment in the evaluated
  AWS testbed setup.

  \item \textbf{Phase 2: Zero-shot does not transfer, explained by three-domain shift.}
  When controlled attacks are launched from the attacker instance under two traffic
  profiles---``clean'' (low-intensity SSH brute-force, Slowloris, and SYN flood at
  200~pps; $434$ flows: $333$ attack, $101$ benign) and ``volumetric'' (high-rate SYN
  flood at 5{,}000~pps plus HTTP and UDP floods; $17{,}319$ flows: $17{,}287$ attack,
  $32$ benign; combined session $17{,}753$ flows)---zero-shot models trained solely on
  offline benchmarks fail to detect live attacks (``clean'' $\mathrm{MCC} = 0.0265$,
  $\mathrm{Recall} = 0.003$, $\mathrm{F1} = 0.006$; ``volumetric'' $\mathrm{MCC} = 0.0007$,
  $\mathrm{Recall} = 0.0003$, $\mathrm{F1} = 0.0006$; held-out split zero-shot
  $\mathrm{MCC} = 0.074$ for UNSW and $-0.217$ for CIC). Inspection of the live flow
  distributions in our laboratory logs reveals the physical root cause: live AWS attack
  sessions (notably persistent Slowloris and SSH brute-force flows kept open across
  NFStream's timeout) exhibit a median flow duration of $64.03$~s compared with the
  offline training mean of $12.18$~s, which depresses directional rate features by three
  to four orders of magnitude (live \texttt{src\_load} median $15.58$ vs offline training
  mean $255{,}117$; live \texttt{dst\_load} median $0.12$ vs offline training mean
  $15{,}260$), while live cloud HTTP responses produce much larger backward packet sizes
  (\texttt{bwd\_mean} median $351$~B on AWS vs $66$~B on CIC and $44$~B on UNSW, yielding
  a single-feature $W_1$ of $1.16$). A three-domain shift analysis over $1.62$M CIC,
  $172{,}684$ UNSW, and $44{,}287$ live AWS flows (Table~\ref{tab:rtshift},
  Figure~\ref{fig:wass})---computed in \texttt{notebooks/21\_dataset\_shift.ipynb} after
  explicit cross-extractor unit alignment (expressing all durations in microseconds,
  dividing UNSW \texttt{sload} by 8 to bytes/s, and recomputing UNSW \texttt{dst\_load} as
  \texttt{dpkts/dur} in packets/s) followed by a single pooled \texttt{StandardScaler}
  (shared $z$-space) and averaging 1D Wasserstein distances
  (\texttt{scipy.stats.wasserstein\_distance}) across the 9 features---confirms that all
  three domains are pairwise separable with domain-classifier accuracy $\ge 0.990$ and
  Proxy $\mathcal{A}$-distance $\ge 1.961$.

  \item \textbf{Few-shot calibration on the AWS testbed: non-monotonic trajectories and
  dependence on target-label representativeness.} Evaluating two distinct splitting
  protocols (Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot}, Figure~\ref{fig:awsfewshot}) reveals that adding
  target labels does not monotonically improve generalization; instead, calibration
  effectiveness depends critically on whether the calibration samples are representative
  of the test traffic. Under the stratified 60\%/40\% flow-level split
  (Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot}a, held-out AWS-test: $7{,}049$ attack and $53$ benign flows,
  where calibration samples cover all attack types present in the session), mixing 1\% of
  target AWS-train labels ($\sim 107$ flows) with UNSW-NB15 lifts MCC from $0.074$
  (zero-shot) to $0.490$, dips slightly to $0.479$ at 5\% ($\sim 532$ flows), and rises to
  $0.588$ at 10\% and $0.596$ at 20\%, matching the AWS-only in-domain reference of
  $0.598$. The CIC+AWS trajectory is more strongly non-monotonic ($-0.217$ at 0\%
  $\rightarrow 0.132$ at 1\% $\rightarrow 0.370$ at 5\% $\rightarrow 0.207$ at 10\%
  $\rightarrow 0.456$ at 20\%), reflecting competition between the large, poorly aligned
  CIC source mass (including the bidirectional vs forward \texttt{src\_load} difference)
  and small target sample variance under heavy test imbalance ($\sim 99\%$ attack, which
  is why we report MCC rather than F1). Under the stricter episode-level split
  (Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot}b, calibrating solely on early \{\texttt{BruteForce, DoS}\}
  episodes plus first-half benign flows, and testing on the unseen late \{\texttt{DDoS}\}
  episode of $121$ attack $+ 83$ benign $= 204$ flows, with 1{,}000-resample bootstrap
  95\% CIs), UNSW zero-shot already achieves $\mathrm{MCC} = 0.579\ [0.46, 0.70]$ (whereas
  CIC zero-shot remains at $-0.220\ [-0.34, -0.08]$), and adding few-shot labels from
  \{\texttt{BruteForce, DoS}\} reduces MCC to $0.523\ [0.40, 0.64]$ at 5\% and
  $0.420\ [0.29, 0.54]$ at 20\% (converging to the episode-trained AWS-only bound of
  $0.418\ [0.29, 0.54]$). This decline occurs because calibrating exclusively on low-rate,
  long-duration BruteForce/Slowloris flows shifts tree splits away from the high-rate
  unseen DDoS regime. The two protocols point to the same condition. Few-shot calibration
  helps when the calibration sample resembles the target evaluation conditions. When it
  does not, it can even degrade transfer to unseen attack regimes.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Reproducibility}
\paragraph{Model, training, and edge efficiency profile.} XGBoost (\texttt{max\_depth}=8,
\texttt{learning\_rate}=0.1, \texttt{n\_estimators}=200,
\texttt{subsample}=\texttt{colsample\_bytree}=0.8), scikit-learn with stratified
train/test splits. Primary metric MCC (robust to class imbalance). Model~A uses the 9 SFM features (7 exact semantic equivalents $+$ 2 documented rate proxies). Benchmarked on a
single CPU core (1~vCPU, single-thread \texttt{nthread}=1, batch size $= 1$ over $1{,}980$ flows; \texttt{model\_efficiency.json}) to reflect an edge sensor or lightweight VM budget, the serialized 9-feature binary model occupies 2.9~MB ($2{,}961.8$~KB), achieves a per-flow inference latency of $439.3~\mu$s mean ($434.2~\mu$s median, SD $22.9~\mu$s), and sustains a directly measured incremental single-core throughput of $2{,}276$~flows/s. Because per-flow tree scoring is independent across flows, multi-core throughput is estimated via linear extrapolation at approximately $18{,}000$--$35{,}000$~flows/s across 8--16 cores (an analytical scaling estimate rather than a direct multi-core measurement).

\paragraph{Class-imbalance handling (no resampling).} (i) metric: MCC throughout;
(ii) cost weighting: live-testbed few-shot models set \texttt{scale\_pos\_weight} to
the negative/positive ratio. We avoid SMOTE/oversampling to keep the cross-network
comparison free of resampling confounds.

\paragraph{No-leakage protocol per experiment.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Baseline (cross-dataset):} z-score fitted on the \emph{source} train
  only; applied transform-only to the target test (model never sees target data).
  \item \emph{Joint training:} z-score fitted on the combined (CIC+UNSW) \emph{train}
  only; evaluated on each held-out test. Used only to show the shared features are
  sufficiently informative; not a deployment setting.
  \item \emph{Few-shot calibration:} given source $\mathcal{S}$ and target split
  $(\mathcal{T}_{\text{tr}},\mathcal{T}_{\text{te}})$, draw a stratified fraction $k$
  of $\mathcal{T}_{\text{tr}}$ (preserving benign/attack ratio), concatenate with the
  full source, fit the z-score scaler \emph{only} on this augmented pool, train, and
  evaluate once on the fixed $\mathcal{T}_{\text{te}}$. Scaler/model never see
  $\mathcal{T}_{\text{te}}$; $k=0$ is zero-shot.
  \item \emph{Mixup:} Beta-interpolation between flows of the two datasets using
  target \emph{train} labels only; same held-out $\mathcal{T}_{\text{te}}$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Multi-seed and confidence intervals.} Offline cross-network results report
mean over 5 seeds with 95\% $t$-intervals ($\mathrm{df}=4$). AWS FAR proportions use
Wilson-score 95\% CIs; the stricter episode-level AWS split uses bootstrap 95\% CIs
(1000 resamples).

\paragraph{Label verification.} On CIC, \texttt{Benign}=0; binary conversion yields
exactly $1{,}348{,}453$ normal and $274{,}808$ attack, so the negative cross-network
MCC is genuine, not a labeling artifact.

\paragraph{AWS cloud testbed protocol (stated transparently).} Region
\texttt{ap-southeast-1}, 2 EC2 instances in an isolated private VPC (attacker +
target/analyzer). Capture via \texttt{tcpdump}$\to$NFStream (9 SFM features), with
duration expressed in microseconds and the two rate features computed once with a
per-second denominator to match the CIC-trained scaler (no secondary rescaling
downstream; see Section~6). Benign-only monitoring accumulates up to $22{,}644$ flows at
6~h (for FAR drift, Table~\ref{tab:far}). The attack session yields $17{,}753$ labeled
flows ($17{,}620$ attack, $133$ benign) across a scheduled timeline
(benign$\to$BruteForce$\to$Slowloris$\to$SYN flood$\to$benign), labeled from the timeline.
AWS-test split: $7{,}049$ attack and $53$ benign (held-out, never trained).
\emph{Limitations of testbed external validity:} (i) traffic is generated within a single
controlled VPC (2 EC2 instances) with timeline-derived labels rather than multi-tenant
production networks; (ii) the benign sample on the attack-session test is small ($53$
flows, $\approx 99\%$ attack), so we report MCC (not F1); and (iii) few-shot recovery
depends on whether calibration samples represent the test attack regime
(Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot}a vs \ref{tab:rtfewshot}b).

\paragraph{Domain-variance computation (context for Table~\ref{tab:dispersion}).} The
per-feature variances are computed in a \emph{single joint} z-score space (both
datasets mapped by one shared scaler, so values are directly comparable); variance
$\approx 0$ means tightly concentrated, $9.5$ means widely spread. Computed over
$1.62$M CIC and $175{,}341$ UNSW flows. This is a descriptive dispersion measure,
distinct from the per-dataset-train z-score used for modeling.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Three findings run together. Single-source NIDS collapse across networks. A directly
measured distribution shift is a major contributor, though feature insufficiency and
other dataset-specific factors may also play a part. Minimal calibration recovers
performance, but only when the calibration labels are representative of the target
environment; we observed this across offline benchmarks and a controlled live AWS cloud
testbed.
\textbf{Limitations:} (a) Model~A combines seven exact semantic equivalents with two
documented rate proxies that are not strictly identical across all three domains. For
\texttt{src\_load} (\texttt{Flow Byts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{sload}), UNSW
$\texttt{sload}/8$ and the live AWS extractor $\texttt{src2dst\_bytes}/\texttt{dur\_s}$
measure unidirectional forward byte/s, whereas CIC \texttt{Flow Byts/s} sums
bidirectional byte/s (coinciding only on unidirectional floods). For \texttt{dst\_load}
(\texttt{Bwd Pkts/s}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{dload}), CIC and AWS measure backward
packet/s while UNSW measures backward bit/s, coupled by variable per-flow packet size
rather than a constant factor. These two proxies exhibit the largest UNSW variances in
joint z-space ($9.00$ and $9.50$, Table~\ref{tab:dispersion}), and we bound our
feature-equivalence claims accordingly. While our subset ablation (Section~3,
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) shows that transfer performance is sensitive to feature
composition---with partial positive transfer at $k=3$ ($+0.377$ on CIC$\rightarrow$UNSW,
where \texttt{dst\_load} is already included) and near-zero transfer across expanded
subsets $k \in \{5, 7, 9\}$---and 1\% target calibration absorbs the scale discrepancy,
future work should re-extract raw PCAPs across all domains with a single unified exporter
to enforce identical directional rate definitions.
(b) Non-monotonicity and representativeness of few-shot calibration: on the AWS testbed,
few-shot calibration is not strictly monotonic on the flow-level split
(Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot}a) and declines on the episode-level split when calibrated
solely on \{\texttt{BruteForce, DoS}\} and tested on unseen \{\texttt{DDoS}\}
(Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot}b, $0.579 \rightarrow 0.420$); furthermore, offline mixup was
evaluated only on seed 42, limiting multi-seed comparisons to the few-shot, CORAL, and
baseline runs.
(c) External validity of the cloud testbed and model scope: the live AWS experiments were
conducted in a controlled two-instance single-region testbed with timeline-based labeling
rather than diverse multi-organization production traffic. Finally, the direction
asymmetry is not fully resolved, and using a single XGBoost is a deliberate
edge-efficiency choice (2.9~MB, $439.3~\mu$s measured single-core latency on 1~vCPU)
rather than a universal model claim; a head-to-head against deep domain-invariant
baselines (DI-NIDS, DANN) needs a different stack and is future work. Adversarial
robustness is deliberately scoped out: preliminary results show adversarial training
improves in-domain evasion robustness but does not close the cross-network gap---confirming
the two problems need different solutions.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
We presented an exploratory, statistics-based cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping
(7 exact semantic equivalents and 2 documented rate proxies) and evaluated it across
offline benchmarks and a controlled live AWS cloud testbed. Single-source models collapse
cross-network (5-seed mean MCC $-0.074$ and $-0.094$) primarily due to distribution shift
(domain-classifier accuracy $\ge 0.990$), while 1\% target labels restores offline
cross-network MCC to $0.659$ and $0.900$. On the AWS cloud testbed, benign false-alarm
rate remains below 0.5\% over 6 hours, and few-shot calibration restores detection from
$0.074$ to $0.490$--$0.596$ when calibration labels are representative of the test traffic
(stratified flow-level split), whereas episode-level evaluation on unseen DDoS shows that
calibrating on non-representative attack types does not improve transfer. Future work:
re-extracting benchmark PCAPs with a unified flow exporter to eliminate rate-proxy
definitional differences; online/streaming adaptation with drift detection across
multi-tenant production networks; automated active-learning/SIEM labeling with a
trusted-holdout guardrail; and multi-class extensions. Adversarial robustness is pursued
in a separate study.
