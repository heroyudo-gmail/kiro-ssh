\begin{abstract}
\noindent Machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) work well on their
training network but often fail on a different one. One cause is easy to overlook:
different flow-export tools compute nominally identical features differently, so a model
trained on one tool can misread another tool's output. We call this a
\emph{feature-extractor mismatch}. To handle it, we build a \emph{Semantic Feature
Mapping} (SFM): a statistics-based audit that checks whether a feature from one tool
truly matches a feature from another in meaning and in scale. Mismatched pairs, such as
the TCP-window features, are rejected, leaving a nine-feature space shared across tools.
Using two public datasets (CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15) with a lightweight XGBoost
detector, a single-source model that is accurate within its own network collapses across
networks; adding only 1\% of labelled target data restores most of the lost accuracy. On
a small, controlled AWS cloud testbed the false-alarm rate stays low, and few-shot
calibration recovers detection provided the calibration data reflect the test traffic.
The cross-network gap is thus largely a distribution-shift problem that can be closed
cheaply once the feature space is made comparable: the best cross-network MCC rises from
near zero to 0.659 and 0.900 with 1\% target labels.

\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} network intrusion detection; cross-network
generalization; semantic feature mapping; few-shot calibration; cloud testbed
validation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cyberattacks impose major and growing economic losses \cite{cremer}, making
machine-learning-based network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) a critical line of
defense \cite{khraisat,buczak}. Such systems routinely report over 99\% accuracy on
public benchmarks, yet this rarely materializes as a working detector on a different,
previously unseen network. The gap stems from a seldom-tested assumption, namely that
training and operational traffic share the same distribution. Prior cross-dataset
studies \cite{cantone,dhooge,verkerken} confirm the collapse, with average MCC falling
from 0.946 within a dataset to 0.294 across datasets \cite{cantone}, but they generally
stop at observing it, and they evaluate only from one dataset to another without ever
testing the mapped model on a live network.

Two deployment-facing problems run through the studies we surveyed, and they are rarely
addressed together. The first is feature-extractor mismatch. Different flow exporters
compute the same nominal feature in different ways, so a unified schema that matches
features by name silently admits pairs that are not comparable. A model trained behind
one tool then misreads the output of another. The second problem is the absence of
validation on a live network. Cross-dataset papers measure the drop from one dataset to
another, but they do not deploy the mapped model on a live cloud testbed, so they never
observe its false-alarm behaviour or the conditions under which calibration actually
helps. These two problems are exactly what an operator faces when moving a benchmark
model into production.

This paper addresses both problems within a single, deployment-oriented recipe. We build
a Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) that does not match features by name but audits each
candidate pair statistically, comparing meaning and scale across tools so that
incompatible pairs, such as the TCP-window features, are rejected rather than silently
accepted. We then carry the audited mapping all the way to a controlled live cloud
testbed on Amazon Web Services (AWS), where we measure the false-alarm rate under benign
traffic, quantify a three-domain distribution shift, and test when a small amount of
target-network labels restores detection. Each of these pieces has appeared separately
in earlier work: unified-feature-set studies \cite{sarhan} standardize schemas but audit
no pair for scale-equivalence and run on no live testbed, while cross-dataset studies
\cite{cantone,dhooge,verkerken} document the collapse but offer neither live validation
nor a low-cost repair. The contribution of this paper is to join these two lines of work
end to end.

Using two deliberately cross-source datasets, CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15, with a
single lightweight XGBoost detector, we show that a model accurate within its own network
collapses across networks, that the collapse is driven mainly by a directly measured
distribution shift rather than by a lack of discriminative features, and that adding only
1\% of labelled target data restores most of the lost accuracy, raising the best
cross-network MCC from near zero to 0.659 and 0.900. On the live AWS testbed the
false-alarm rate stays low under benign traffic, confirming that the mapping introduces
no scaling defect, and few-shot calibration recovers detection as long as the calibration
data reflect the test traffic, an observation we report honestly together with the cases
where the benefit is smaller. All reported values come from executed, reproducible
experiments; no illustrative numbers are presented as results, and non-monotone outcomes
are reported as observed.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on
cross-dataset generalization, unified feature sets, dataset integrity, and domain
adaptation. Section 3 presents the proposed method, describing the two cross-source
datasets and the construction and statistical audit of the Semantic Feature Mapping.
Section 4 reports the performance evaluation: the cross-network baseline and diagnosis,
the low-cost recovery with minimal target labels, the validation on the live AWS cloud
testbed, the experimental setup and reproducibility protocol, and a discussion of the
findings and limitations. Section 5 concludes and outlines future work.

\section{Literature Review}
Machine-learning NIDS span tree ensembles and deep models, from deep and
transformer-based detectors \cite{shone,wu} to XGBoost variants on UNSW-NB15
\cite{husain}, and most of them report strong in-domain results that do not by
themselves guarantee robustness when the detector is moved to a different network.
This review groups the studies most relevant to our problem into four strands and, at
the end, states precisely what our work adds to the closest among them.

The first strand concerns cross-dataset generalization. D'Hooge et al.\ \cite{dhooge}
showed that supervised NIDS fail on unseen networks, Verkerken et al.\ \cite{verkerken}
reported roughly a 30\% drop in AUROC with unsupervised models, and Cantone et al.\
\cite{cantone} documented MCC falling from 0.946 within a dataset to 0.294 across four
datasets. Together these works establish that the cross-network collapse is real and
systematic, but they largely stop at observing it; none separates whether the failure
comes from insufficient features or from a distribution shift, and none offers a
low-cost recovery or a validation on a live network.

A second strand aims to make datasets comparable through a unified feature set. Sarhan
et al.\ \cite{sarhan,sarhannf} proposed a standardized NetFlow feature set so that
models can be trained and tested across datasets. Our approach goes one step further:
rather than trusting that identically named features are equivalent, we audit each
mapped pair with a statistics-based scale-equivalence check on its original scale and
reject pairs that merely share a name, such as the TCP-window features, which a
name-based schema would otherwise admit silently.

A third strand examines the integrity of the benchmarks themselves, which matters
because an apparent cross-network gap could instead be an artefact of noisy labels.
Reconstruction studies found that over 20\% of CIC-IDS2017 flows needed relabeling
\cite{engelen}, identified feature-computation errors in CICFlowMeter \cite{rosay,lanvin},
and reported label-error rates of 6--7\% on the CIC and CSE-CIC datasets \cite{liu};
Catillo et al.\ \cite{catillo} further questioned whether reported benchmark progress
reflects genuine advances. Most directly relevant to us, Layeghy et al.\ \cite{layeghy}
showed that synthetic benign traffic differs statistically from real networks by a large
Wasserstein distance, which motivates both our cross-tool mapping and our decision to
validate on a live cloud testbed rather than on another synthetic dataset.

A fourth strand addresses domain adaptation. CORAL \cite{coral} aligns feature
covariance, and few-shot methods target label-limited NIDS, while deep
domain-invariant detectors learn adversarially invariant representations but require a
heavy deep stack and ample target data, which is impractical for edge deployment. We
instead keep a fixed lightweight XGBoost \cite{xgboost} backbone and recover performance
through inexpensive per-deployment calibration, a deliberately different, edge-oriented
operating point; a head-to-head comparison against deep domain adaptation would require
a different stack and is left as future work rather than claimed as an unmeasured
advantage.

We do not claim that any single one of these components is new. What we claim is the
combination, and Table~\ref{tab:related} states what we add to the nearest studies; the
comparison is made against the studies we surveyed rather than as an absolute first.

carry the whole recipe end to end onto a live cloud testbed, measuring the false-alarm
rate, quantifying a three-domain shift, and testing few-shot repair.

\section{Proposed Method}
This section describes the two datasets used as deliberately cross-source networks and
the construction of the Semantic Feature Mapping that makes their feature spaces
comparable, which together form the proposed cross-network detection recipe.

\subsection{Datasets}
We use two public benchmark datasets that were deliberately produced by different flow
exporters, so that the feature-extractor mismatch we study appears naturally rather than

\subsection{Semantic Feature Mapping}
The two datasets are produced by different flow exporters and therefore do not share a
common feature set. The Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) solves this by keeping only the
features that are genuinely comparable across both tools, that is, the intersection of
the two schemas. Figure~\ref{fig:sfm} illustrates this idea: out of 68 features in
CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and 42 in UNSW-NB15, nine features carry the same meaning in both and
form the shared SFM feature space used throughout the paper, while the remaining
features are tool-specific and are not used.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figure-q1/fig_sfm_intersection.png}
\caption{Intersection of Semantic Feature Mapping.}
\label{fig:sfm}
\end{figure}

Deciding which features are truly comparable is not done by name alone, because the same
name can hide different units or scales. Instead, each candidate pair passes a short,
statistics-based audit. Since the CIC features in our release are z-scored, we first
return them to their original units with Eq.~\eqref{eq:unscale}, where $\mu$ and $\sigma$
are the stored mean and standard deviation.

\begin{equation}
  x_{\mathrm{orig}} = x_{\mathrm{scaled}} \cdot \sigma + \mu
  \label{eq:unscale}
\end{equation}

We then compare the two tools on the same scale. For each pair we take the ratio of the
median values and of the 99th percentile (p99) values between the two datasets, and
multiply by a unit factor $c$ that accounts for documented unit differences, as in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:ratios}. The factor is $c=10^{-6}$ for durations in microseconds versus
seconds, $c=10^{-3}$ for inter-arrival times in microseconds versus milliseconds, $c=8$
for byte-versus-bit rates, and $c=1$ when the units already match.

\begin{equation}
  r'_{\mathrm{med}} = c\,\frac{\mathrm{med}_{\mathrm{CIC}}}{\mathrm{med}_{\mathrm{UNSW}}},
  \qquad
  r'_{\mathrm{p99}} = c\,\frac{\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{CIC}}}{\mathrm{p99}_{\mathrm{UNSW}}}
  \label{eq:ratios}
\end{equation}

A pair is accepted as comparable when, after the unit correction, both ratios stay
within one order of magnitude of unity, as stated in Eq.~\eqref{eq:verdict}; otherwise
the pair is treated as a scale mismatch or, when the two features are fundamentally
different, rejected.

\begin{equation}
  \max\!\left(|\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{med}}|,\; |\log_{10} r'_{\mathrm{p99}}|\right) \le 1
  \label{eq:verdict}
\end{equation}

Applying this audit to all candidate pairs (full details in Table~\ref{tab:sfm}) yields
nine shared features: \texttt{dur}, \texttt{spkts}, \texttt{dpkts}, \texttt{sbytes},
\texttt{dbytes}, \texttt{smean}, \texttt{dmean}, \texttt{sload}, and \texttt{dload}.
Seven of them (packet and byte counts, mean packet sizes, and duration) are exact
equivalents across both tools, while the two rate features \texttt{sload} and
\texttt{dload} are retained as documented proxies because the two tools report rates with
slightly different directional scope or units; we flag this as an explicit limitation.
The clearest rejected case is the TCP-window pair, which is continuous in CICFlowMeter
but effectively binary in Argus/Bro: despite the shared name, the two are not comparable
and are discarded. The z-score scaler used for modeling is fit on the training split only
to avoid leakage.

\section{Performance Evaluation}
This section evaluates the proposed method. We first establish the cross-network
collapse and diagnose its cause, then show a low-cost recovery, validate the recipe on
a live AWS cloud testbed, document the experimental setup and reproducibility protocol,
and finally discuss the findings and limitations.

\subsection{Cross-Network Baseline and Diagnosis}
A single XGBoost detector performs very well within each network (MCC around 0.91 on CIC
and 0.74 on UNSW) but collapses to near zero when it is tested on the other network, as
shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gap}. Because this is measured on the SFM-aligned feature space,
the collapse cannot be blamed on a trivial schema mismatch. Throughout the paper we use
the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as the primary metric, since it remains
reliable under the heavy class imbalance typical of intrusion data. The MCC is defined in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:mcc}, where TP, TN, FP, and FN denote the true positives, true negatives,
false positives, and false negatives.

\begin{equation}
  \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}
  {\sqrt{(TP{+}FP)(TP{+}FN)(TN{+}FP)(TN{+}FN)}}
  \label{eq:mcc}
\end{equation}

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} reports the detection quality in each scenario for the two
feature sets, Model~A (nine SFM features) and Model~B (the eleven features that also
include the two inter-arrival-time pairs). When the model is trained and tested on the
same network, both feature sets reach high MCC. When it is trained on one network and
tested on the other, in either direction, the MCC drops to about zero, meaning the
detector is no better than random guessing on the unseen network. The resulting
generalization gap of roughly 0.8 to 0.99 is almost identical for Model~A and Model~B,
which shows that simply adding more features does not help the model transfer across
networks. Figure~\ref{fig:gap} visualizes this contrast between the strong in-domain
performance and the near-zero cross-network performance.

\label{fig:gap}

To understand why the detector fails across networks, we ask whether the shared features
simply lack enough information or whether the two networks differ in distribution. When a
single model is trained jointly on both networks, it performs well on each of them (mean
MCC about 0.913 on CIC and 0.730 on UNSW), which shows that the nine SFM features do
carry enough discriminative information. Feature insufficiency is therefore not the main
cause. Unsupervised covariance alignment with CORAL \cite{coral} also fails to recover
cross-network detection, because aligning only the first- and second-order moments
without any target labels does not move the decision boundary to the right place when the
two networks differ in their attack composition (Figure~\ref{fig:align}).

The evidence instead points to a distribution shift between the two networks. A simple
domain classifier can tell CIC and UNSW apart with about 0.99 accuracy, and the average
distance between their feature distributions is large, which directly confirms that the
two networks occupy different regions of the feature space. This is consistent with the
Ben-David bound in Eq.~\eqref{eq:bendavid}, where a larger divergence between source and
target limits the achievable cross-network performance. We therefore conclude that the
distribution shift is the major contributor to the failure, while acknowledging that
other dataset-specific factors, such as different label taxonomies, attack composition,
and the two rate-proxy approximations, may also play a part.

\begin{equation}
  \epsilon_T(h) \le \epsilon_S(h) + d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(S,T) + \lambda
  \label{eq:bendavid}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Low-Cost Recovery}
Having identified the distribution shift as the main cause of the collapse, we now show
that it can be corrected at a very low labeling cost. Table~\ref{tab:fewshot} reports the
cross-network MCC when the detector is given only a small fraction of labelled data from
the target network. Adding just 1\% of target labels is enough to bring the detector back
from near-random behaviour to a level close to a model trained jointly on both networks,
and increasing the budget to 5\% or 10\% adds little further improvement. Cross-dataset
mixup, which reuses only the target training labels, recovers performance to a similar
degree. In short, once the feature space is made comparable through SFM, a handful of
target labels is sufficient to restore detection, which is practical for a real
deployment.

One asymmetry is worth noting: adapting toward UNSW recovers more fully than adapting
toward CIC. This is consistent with the UNSW network having a wider spread in its rate
and packet-size features, so a model calibrated in that direction generalizes more
easily. We present this as an observation supported by the data rather than a proven
cause. Figure~\ref{fig:fewshot} shows the recovery curve as the fraction of target labels
increases, where most of the gain already appears at 1\%.

\subsection{Controlled Cloud Testbed Validation on AWS}
We evaluate the SFM-aligned detector on a controlled AWS cloud testbed (two EC2
instances in \texttt{ap-southeast-1} inside an isolated private VPC, capturing raw
packets via \texttt{tcpdump} into NFStream to extract the 9 SFM features), a live-testbed
step omitted by the surveyed cross-dataset studies (Table~\ref{tab:related}). We frame
this experiment as a controlled validation on a live testbed rather than a broad
production deployment: all traffic is generated between two instances in a single VPC,
and the attack ground truth comes from the scheduled execution timeline. Before testing,
we align the nine SFM features from NFStream to the CIC-trained scaler, mainly by
expressing duration in the same unit as CICFlowMeter and computing the rate features per
second. The seven exact-equivalent features transfer directly, while the two rate proxies
carry the small directional differences already noted in Section~3, which we keep in mind
when interpreting the results.

We first check the detector on benign testbed traffic. Over three cumulative windows of
3~minutes, 1~hour, and 6~hours (Table~\ref{tab:far}), the false-alarm rate stays below
0.5\% and does not drift upward over time. This low and stable false-alarm rate indicates
that the unit alignment works correctly in the live setting, since a scaling error would
instead have produced a flood of false alarms.

We then launch controlled attacks and test the offline-trained model directly, without
any calibration. In this zero-shot setting the detector essentially fails to flag the
live attacks. Inspecting the captured flows explains why: live attack sessions, such as
persistent Slowloris and SSH brute-force connections, stay open much longer than the
flows seen during offline training, which pushes the rate features far below their
training range, and live cloud responses produce noticeably larger backward packets.
In other words, the live network differs from both benchmarks in distribution. This is
confirmed in Figure~\ref{fig:wass}, where all three domains (CIC, UNSW, and the live AWS
traffic) are clearly separated from one another.

Finally, we add a small fraction of labelled AWS traffic and recalibrate.
Table~\ref{tab:rtfewshot} and Figure~\ref{fig:awsfewshot} show that calibration helps,
but only when the labelled samples resemble the traffic being tested. When the few-shot
labels cover the same attack types as the test set, a small budget already recovers
detection to a level close to a model trained entirely on AWS. The opposite case is
equally informative: in a stricter test where we calibrate only on early BruteForce and
DoS traffic and then evaluate on an unseen DDoS attack, adding more labels actually lowers
the MCC (from about 0.58 down to 0.42), because the model is tuned toward the wrong attack
regime. The practical message is simple: a few target labels restore detection as long as
they are representative of what the detector will actually face.

\subsection{Experimental Setup and Reproducibility}
The detector is a single XGBoost model trained with standard settings (depth 8, learning
rate 0.1, 200 trees, and 0.8 subsampling) on the nine SFM features, using MCC as the
primary metric because it remains reliable under heavy class imbalance. The model is
deliberately lightweight so that it can run on an edge sensor or a small virtual machine:
the serialized model is only a few megabytes and classifies each flow in well under a
millisecond on a single CPU core, which is more than fast enough for real-time use. We
do not apply any oversampling such as SMOTE; instead, where needed, the few-shot models
simply weight the positive class by the negative-to-positive ratio, so that the
cross-network comparison is not confounded by resampling.

All experiments follow a strict no-leakage protocol. In the cross-dataset baseline the
z-score scaler is fit on the source training data only and then applied to the target
test set, so the model never sees any target data. In joint training the scaler is fit
on the combined training data and evaluated on each held-out test set; this setting is
used only to confirm that the shared features are informative, not as a deployment
scenario. In few-shot calibration we add a small, class-balanced fraction of the target
training data to the source, fit the scaler only on this combined pool, and evaluate once
on a fixed held-out target test set that the model never sees; a zero fraction reduces to
the zero-shot case. Cross-dataset mixup follows the same held-out evaluation but builds
interpolated samples using only the target training labels. Offline cross-network results
are averaged over five seeds with 95\% confidence intervals, the AWS false-alarm rates
use Wilson-score intervals, and the stricter unseen-attack AWS evaluation uses bootstrap
intervals.

For the live AWS testbed, two EC2 instances (an attacker and a target/analyzer) run inside
an isolated private VPC, and traffic is captured with tcpdump and processed by NFStream to
produce the nine SFM features, with durations and rates expressed in the same units as the
CIC-trained scaler. Benign-only monitoring accumulates tens of thousands of flows for the
false-alarm measurement, while a scheduled attack session (moving through benign,
brute-force, Slowloris, and SYN-flood phases) provides the labelled attack traffic, from
which a held-out test set is reserved and never used for training. We note the limits of
this testbed for external validity: the traffic comes from a single controlled VPC with
labels derived from the execution timeline rather than from a multi-tenant production
network, the benign portion of the attack test is small (so we report MCC rather than F1),
and, as shown earlier, the benefit of few-shot calibration depends on whether the
calibration samples represent the attacks seen at test time.

\subsection{Discussion and Limitations}
Taken together, the experiments tell a consistent story. A detector trained on a single
network collapses when moved to another, the main reason is a measurable distribution
shift between the networks rather than a lack of informative features, and a small amount
of target labels restores detection as long as those labels resemble the traffic the
detector will actually face. This pattern holds both on the offline benchmarks and on the
live AWS testbed.

The study also has clear limitations. Two of the nine SFM features are rate proxies that
are not strictly identical across the three tools, and Table~\ref{tab:dispersion} shows
that these two features are exactly the ones with the widest spread in UNSW, which is
consistent with the direction asymmetry observed earlier; a cleaner solution would be to
re-extract all datasets with a single flow exporter so that the rate definitions match
exactly. The benefit of few-shot calibration is also not guaranteed: as shown on the AWS
testbed, it can even decrease when the calibration labels come from attack types that
differ from those at test time. Finally, the live experiments were run in a controlled
two-instance testbed rather than a diverse production network, and we deliberately keep a
single lightweight model for edge efficiency rather than claiming it is the best possible
detector; a comparison against heavier deep domain-adaptation methods is left for future
work. Adversarial robustness is likewise out of scope here, since our preliminary results
indicate that it addresses a different problem and does not close the cross-network gap.

Table~\ref{tab:dispersion} reports how widely each SFM feature varies within each dataset,
measured in a shared z-score space so the two datasets are directly comparable. The
feature values in UNSW are, on average, far more spread out than in CIC, and this larger
dispersion helps explain why adapting toward UNSW recovers more fully than adapting toward
CIC.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
This paper studied why a network intrusion detector that works well on one network often
fails on another, and how to fix it at a low cost. We showed that two datasets built with
different flow-export tools can be made comparable through a simple, statistics-based
Semantic Feature Mapping that keeps only the features that truly match across tools. Using
this shared feature space, a single lightweight model is accurate within each network but
collapses across networks, and we traced this collapse to a genuine distribution shift
between the networks rather than to a lack of useful features. The gap can then be closed
cheaply: adding only a small fraction of labelled data from the new network restores
detection to a level close to full retraining. Crucially, we confirmed the whole recipe on
a controlled cloud testbed, where the detector kept a low false-alarm rate on normal traffic and
recovered its detection ability once a few representative labels from the new environment
were provided.

For future work, a natural next step is to study the detector under attack rather than
only under normal distribution shift. This includes evasion attacks, where an adversary
slightly alters traffic to avoid detection, and adversarial training as a possible
defence, so that robustness and cross-network generalization can be examined together.
Other promising directions are continuous, online adaptation that keeps the model up to
date as traffic drifts over time, automated labelling to reduce the manual effort of
calibration, extending the detector from a simple attack-or-benign decision to recognizing
specific attack categories, and validating the approach on larger and more diverse
real-world networks beyond the controlled testbed used here.
