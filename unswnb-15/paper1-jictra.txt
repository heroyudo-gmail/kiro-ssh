TITLE
-----
Semantic Feature Mapping for Cross-Network Intrusion Detection: Validation on a Controlled Cloud Testbed

AUTHORS
-------
Hero Yudo Martono (corresponding author), Iwan Syarif, Ferry Astika Saputra

Affiliation: Department of Informatics and Computer Engineering, Politeknik
Elektronika Negeri Surabaya (PENS), Surabaya, Indonesia
Email: hero@pens.ac.id; iwanarif@pens.ac.id; ferryas@pens.ac.id

ABSTRACT
--------
Machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) work well on their training
network but often fail on a different one. One cause is easy to overlook: different
flow-export tools compute nominally identical features differently, so a model trained on
one tool can misread another tool's output. We call this a feature-extractor mismatch. To
handle it, we build a Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM): a statistics-based audit that checks
whether a feature from one tool truly matches a feature from another in meaning and in
scale. Mismatched pairs, such as the TCP-window features, are rejected, leaving a
nine-feature space shared across tools. Using two public datasets (CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and
UNSW-NB15) with a lightweight XGBoost detector, a single-source model that is accurate
within its own network collapses across networks; adding only 1% of labelled target data
restores most of the lost accuracy. On a small, controlled AWS cloud testbed the
false-alarm rate stays low, and few-shot calibration recovers detection provided the
calibration data reflect the test traffic. The cross-network gap is thus largely a
distribution-shift problem that can be closed cheaply once the feature space is made
comparable: the best cross-network MCC rises from near zero to 0.659 and 0.900 with 1%
target labels.

Keywords: network intrusion detection; cross-network generalization; semantic feature
mapping; few-shot calibration; cloud testbed validation.

1. INTRODUCTION
---------------
Cyberattacks impose major and growing economic losses [1], making machine-learning-based
network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) a critical line of defence [2,3]. Such systems
routinely report over 99% accuracy on public benchmarks, yet this rarely materializes as
a working detector on a different, previously unseen network. The gap stems from a
seldom-tested assumption, namely that training and operational traffic share the same
distribution. Prior cross-dataset studies [4,5,6] confirm the collapse, with average MCC
falling from 0.946 within a dataset to 0.294 across datasets [4], but they generally stop
at observing it, and they evaluate only from one dataset to another without ever testing
the mapped model on a live network.

Two deployment-facing problems run through the studies we surveyed, and they are rarely
addressed together. The first is feature-extractor mismatch. Different flow exporters
compute the same nominal feature in different ways, so a unified schema that matches
features by name silently admits pairs that are not comparable. A model trained behind
one tool then misreads the output of another. The second problem is the absence of
validation on a live network. Cross-dataset papers measure the drop from one dataset to
another, but they do not deploy the mapped model on a live cloud testbed, so they never
observe its false-alarm behaviour or the conditions under which calibration actually
helps. These two problems are exactly what an operator faces when moving a benchmark
model into production.

This paper addresses both problems within a single, deployment-oriented recipe. We build
a Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) that does not match features by name but audits each
candidate pair statistically, comparing meaning and scale across tools so that
incompatible pairs, such as the TCP-window features, are rejected rather than silently
accepted. We then carry the audited mapping all the way to a controlled cloud testbed on
Amazon Web Services (AWS), where we measure the false-alarm rate under benign traffic,
quantify a three-domain distribution shift, and test when a small amount of target-network
labels restores detection. Each of these pieces has appeared separately in earlier work:
unified-feature-set studies [7] standardize schemas but audit no pair for scale-equivalence
and run on no live testbed, while cross-dataset studies [4,5,6] document the collapse but
offer neither live validation nor a low-cost repair. The contribution of this paper is to
join these two lines of work end to end.

Using two deliberately cross-source datasets, CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15, with a single
lightweight XGBoost detector, we show that a model accurate within its own network
collapses across networks, that the collapse is driven mainly by a directly measured
distribution shift rather than by a lack of discriminative features, and that adding only
1% of labelled target data restores most of the lost accuracy, raising the best
cross-network MCC from near zero to 0.659 and 0.900. On the AWS testbed the false-alarm
rate stays low under benign traffic, confirming that the mapping introduces no scaling
defect, and few-shot calibration recovers detection as long as the calibration data
reflect the test traffic, an observation we report honestly together with the cases where
the benefit is smaller. All reported values come from executed, reproducible experiments;
no illustrative numbers are presented as results, and non-monotone outcomes are reported
as observed.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews the literature on
cross-dataset generalization, unified feature sets, dataset integrity, and domain
adaptation. Section 3 presents the proposed method, describing the two cross-source
datasets and the construction and statistical audit of the Semantic Feature Mapping.
Section 4 reports the performance evaluation: the cross-network baseline and diagnosis,
the low-cost recovery with minimal target labels, the validation on the controlled AWS
cloud testbed, the experimental setup and reproducibility protocol, and a discussion of
the findings and limitations. Section 5 concludes and outlines future work.


2. LITERATURE REVIEW
--------------------
Machine-learning NIDS span tree ensembles and deep models, from deep and
transformer-based detectors [8,9] to XGBoost variants on UNSW-NB15 [10], and most of them
report strong in-domain results that do not by themselves guarantee robustness when the
detector is moved to a different network. This review groups the studies most relevant to
our problem into four strands and, at the end, states precisely what our work adds to the
closest among them.

The first strand concerns cross-dataset generalization. D'Hooge et al. [5] showed that
supervised NIDS fail on unseen networks, Verkerken et al. [6] reported roughly a 30% drop
in AUROC with unsupervised models, and Cantone et al. [4] documented MCC falling from
0.946 within a dataset to 0.294 across four datasets. Together these works establish that
the cross-network collapse is real and systematic, but they largely stop at observing it;
none separates whether the failure comes from insufficient features or from a distribution
shift, and none offers a low-cost recovery or a validation on a live network.

A second strand aims to make datasets comparable through a unified feature set. Sarhan et
al. [7,11] proposed a standardized NetFlow feature set so that models can be trained and
tested across datasets. Our approach goes one step further: rather than trusting that
identically named features are equivalent, we audit each mapped pair with a
statistics-based scale-equivalence check on its original scale and reject pairs that
merely share a name, such as the TCP-window features, which a name-based schema would
otherwise admit silently.

A third strand examines the integrity of the benchmarks themselves, which matters because
an apparent cross-network gap could instead be an artefact of noisy labels. Reconstruction
studies found that over 20% of CIC-IDS2017 flows needed relabeling [12], identified
feature-computation errors in CICFlowMeter [13,14], and reported label-error rates of 6-7%
on the CIC and CSE-CIC datasets [15]; Catillo et al. [16] further questioned whether
reported benchmark progress reflects genuine advances. Most directly relevant to us,
Layeghy et al. [17] showed that synthetic benign traffic differs statistically from real
networks by a large Wasserstein distance, which motivates both our cross-tool mapping and
our decision to validate on a live cloud testbed rather than on another synthetic dataset.

A fourth strand addresses domain adaptation. CORAL [18] aligns feature covariance, and
few-shot methods target label-limited NIDS, while deep domain-invariant detectors learn
adversarially invariant representations but require a heavy deep stack and ample target
data, which is impractical for edge deployment. We instead keep a fixed lightweight
XGBoost [19] backbone and recover performance through inexpensive per-deployment
calibration, a deliberately different, edge-oriented operating point; a head-to-head
comparison against deep domain adaptation would require a different stack and is left as
future work rather than claimed as an unmeasured advantage.

We do not claim that any single one of these components is new. What we claim is the
combination, and Table 1 states what we add to the nearest studies; the comparison is made
against the studies we surveyed rather than as an absolute first.

TABLE 1. Comparison with related NIDS studies.
  Study                   Evaluation scope        Cross-tool method   Live test  Low-label repair
  Sarhan et al. [7,11]    Cross-dataset           Name-based schema   No         No
  Cantone et al. [4]      Cross-dataset           Common features     No         No
  D'Hooge et al. [5]      Cross-dataset           Common features     No         No
  Verkerken et al. [6]    Cross-dataset           Common features     No         No
  Layeghy et al. [17]     Single-network          Deep invariant      No         No
  Husain et al. [10]      Single-network          None                No         No
  This work               Cross-dataset + live    SFM audit           Yes        Yes

Relative to unified feature sets [7,11], we add a statistics-based compatibility audit
that rejects same-named but non-comparable pairs such as the TCP-window features, rather
than relying on a shared schema alone. Relative to the cross-dataset studies [4,5,6], we
add a controlled diagnosis that separates feature insufficiency from distribution shift
together with a low-label repair, rather than only documenting the drop. And relative to
the synthetic-versus-real shift measurement of Layeghy et al. [17], we carry the whole
recipe end to end onto a live cloud testbed, measuring the false-alarm rate, quantifying a
three-domain shift, and testing few-shot repair.


3. PROPOSED METHOD
------------------
This section describes the two datasets used as deliberately cross-source networks and the
construction of the Semantic Feature Mapping that makes their feature spaces comparable,
which together form the proposed cross-network detection recipe.

3.1 DATASETS
------------
We use two public benchmark datasets that were deliberately produced by different flow
exporters, so that the feature-extractor mismatch we study appears naturally rather than
being introduced artificially. CSE-CIC-IDS2018 was generated with CICFlowMeter, while
UNSW-NB15 was generated with Argus and Bro/Zeek. Their main characteristics are summarized
in Table 2. For a controlled binary setting, all attack types are collapsed into a single
attack class, and detection is treated as a benign-versus-attack problem throughout.

TABLE 2. Datasets used in this study.

3.2 SEMANTIC FEATURE MAPPING
----------------------------
The two datasets are produced by different flow exporters and therefore do not share a
common feature set. The Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) solves this by keeping only the
features that are genuinely comparable across both tools, that is, the intersection of the
two schemas. Figure 1 illustrates this idea: out of 68 features in CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and 42
in UNSW-NB15, nine features carry the same meaning in both and form the shared SFM feature
space used throughout the paper, while the remaining features are tool-specific and are not
used.

[FIGURE 1] file: figure-q1/fig_sfm_intersection.png
Caption: Intersection of Semantic Feature Mapping.

Deciding which features are truly comparable is not done by name alone, because the same
name can hide different units or scales. Instead, each candidate pair passes a short,
statistics-based audit. Since the CIC features in our release are z-scored, we first return
them to their original units with Eq. 1, where mu and sigma are the stored mean and
standard deviation.

We then compare the two tools on the same scale. For each pair we take the ratio of the
median values and of the 99th percentile (p99) values between the two datasets, and
multiply by a unit factor c that accounts for documented unit differences, as in Eq. 2.
The factor is c = 10^-6 for durations in microseconds versus seconds, c = 10^-3 for
inter-arrival times in microseconds versus milliseconds, c = 8 for byte-versus-bit rates,
and c = 1 when the units already match.

A pair is accepted as comparable when, after the unit correction, both ratios stay within
one order of magnitude of unity, as stated in Eq. 3; otherwise the pair is treated as a
scale mismatch or, when the two features are fundamentally different, rejected.

Applying this audit to all candidate pairs (full details in Table 3) yields nine shared
features: dur, spkts, dpkts, sbytes, dbytes, smean, dmean, sload, and dload. Seven of them
(packet and byte counts, mean packet sizes, and duration) are exact equivalents across both
tools, while the two rate features sload and dload are retained as documented proxies
because the two tools report rates with slightly different directional scope or units; we
flag this as an explicit limitation. The clearest rejected case is the TCP-window pair,
which is continuous in CICFlowMeter but effectively binary in Argus/Bro: despite the shared
name, the two are not comparable and are discarded. The z-score scaler used for modeling is
fit on the training split only to avoid leakage.

  TotLen Fwd Pkts    sbytes         Model A (aligned)
  TotLen Bwd Pkts    dbytes         Model A (aligned)

  Bwd IAT Mean       dinpkt         Model B (optional)
  Init Fwd Win Byts  swin           Discarded (mismatch)

We also examined empirically how much the two rate proxies affect the results; the detailed
per-subset numbers are in the feature-count ablation of Table 4. In the joint model,
dst_load is among the most discriminative features and enters the ranking early, whereas
src_load enters only in the full nine-feature set. The ablation also shows that
cross-network transfer is sensitive to the feature subset in a non-monotonic way: a very
small subset can give partial positive transfer in one direction, while larger subsets
improve in-domain and joint accuracy yet leave cross-network transfer near zero. Because
the near-zero transfer already appears in subsets that exclude src_load, the cross-network
collapse cannot be attributed to either rate proxy alone.

4. PERFORMANCE EVALUATION
-------------------------
This section evaluates the proposed method. We first establish the cross-network collapse
and diagnose its cause, then show a low-cost recovery, validate the recipe on a controlled
AWS cloud testbed, document the experimental setup and reproducibility protocol, and
finally discuss the findings and limitations.

4.1 CROSS-NETWORK BASELINE AND DIAGNOSIS
----------------------------------------
A single XGBoost detector performs very well within each network (MCC around 0.91 on CIC
and 0.74 on UNSW) but collapses to near zero when it is tested on the other network, as
shown in Figure 2. Because this is measured on the SFM-aligned feature space, the collapse
cannot be blamed on a trivial schema mismatch. Throughout the paper we use the Matthews
Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as the primary metric, since it remains reliable under the
heavy class imbalance typical of intrusion data. The MCC is defined in Eq. 4, where TP, TN,
FP, and FN denote the true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives.

TABLE 5. Cross-dataset test results (MCC).
  Scenario                        Model A    Model B
  Same-CIC (train & test CIC)     0.913      0.914
  Same-UNSW (train & test UNSW)   0.745      0.743
  CIC -> UNSW                     -0.072     0.004
  UNSW -> CIC                     -0.061     -0.046
  Generalization gap              0.81-0.99  0.79-0.91

Table 5 reports the detection quality in each scenario for the two feature sets, Model A
(nine SFM features) and Model B (the eleven features that also include the two
inter-arrival-time pairs). When the model is trained and tested on the same network, both
feature sets reach high MCC. When it is trained on one network and tested on the other, in
either direction, the MCC drops to about zero, meaning the detector is no better than
random guessing on the unseen network. The resulting generalization gap of roughly 0.8 to
0.99 is almost identical for Model A and Model B, which shows that simply adding more
features does not help the model transfer across networks. Figure 2 visualizes this
contrast between the strong in-domain performance and the near-zero cross-network
performance.

[FIGURE 2] file: figure-q1/fig1_generalization_gap.png
Caption: Cross-network generalization gap (MCC).

To understand why the detector fails across networks, we ask whether the shared features
simply lack enough information or whether the two networks differ in distribution. When a
single model is trained jointly on both networks, it performs well on each of them (mean
MCC about 0.913 on CIC and 0.730 on UNSW), which shows that the nine SFM features do carry
enough discriminative information. Feature insufficiency is therefore not the main cause.
Unsupervised covariance alignment with CORAL [18] also fails to recover cross-network
detection, because aligning only the first- and second-order moments without any target
labels does not move the decision boundary to the right place when the two networks differ
in their attack composition (Figure 3).

The evidence instead points to a distribution shift between the two networks. A simple
domain classifier can tell CIC and UNSW apart with about 0.99 accuracy, and the average
distance between their feature distributions is large, which directly confirms that the two
networks occupy different regions of the feature space. This is consistent with the
Ben-David bound in Eq. 5, where a larger divergence between source and target limits the
achievable cross-network performance. We therefore conclude that the distribution shift is
the major contributor to the failure, while acknowledging that other dataset-specific
factors, such as different label taxonomies, attack composition, and the two rate-proxy
approximations, may also play a part.

4.2 LOW-COST RECOVERY
---------------------
Having identified the distribution shift as the main cause of the collapse, we now show
that it can be corrected at a very low labeling cost. Table 6 reports the cross-network MCC
when the detector is given only a small fraction of labelled data from the target network.
Adding just 1% of target labels is enough to bring the detector back from near-random
behaviour to a level close to a model trained jointly on both networks, and increasing the
budget to 5% or 10% adds little further improvement. Cross-dataset mixup, which reuses only
the target training labels, recovers performance to a similar degree. In short, once the
feature space is made comparable through SFM, a handful of target labels is sufficient to
restore detection, which is practical for a real deployment.

One asymmetry is worth noting: adapting toward UNSW recovers more fully than adapting toward
CIC. This is consistent with the UNSW network having a wider spread in its rate and
packet-size features, so a model calibrated in that direction generalizes more easily. We
present this as an observation supported by the data rather than a proven cause. Figure 4
shows the recovery curve as the fraction of target labels increases, where most of the gain
already appears at 1%.

4.3 CONTROLLED CLOUD TESTBED VALIDATION ON AWS
----------------------------------------------
We evaluate the SFM-aligned detector on a controlled AWS cloud testbed (two EC2 instances
in ap-southeast-1 inside an isolated private VPC, capturing raw packets via tcpdump into
NFStream to extract the 9 SFM features), a live-testbed step omitted by the surveyed
cross-dataset studies (Table 1). We frame this experiment as a controlled validation on a
live testbed rather than a broad production deployment: all traffic is generated between
two instances in a single VPC, and the attack ground truth comes from the scheduled
execution timeline. Before testing, we align the nine SFM features from NFStream to the
CIC-trained scaler, mainly by expressing duration in the same unit as CICFlowMeter and
computing the rate features per second. The seven exact-equivalent features transfer
directly, while the two rate proxies carry the small directional differences already noted
in Section 3, which we keep in mind when interpreting the results.

We first check the detector on benign testbed traffic. Over three cumulative windows of 3
minutes, 1 hour, and 6 hours (Table 7), the false-alarm rate stays below 0.5% and does not
drift upward over time. This low and stable false-alarm rate indicates that the unit
alignment works correctly in the live setting, since a scaling error would instead have
produced a flood of false alarms.

We then launch controlled attacks and test the offline-trained model directly, without any
calibration. In this zero-shot setting the detector essentially fails to flag the live
attacks. Inspecting the captured flows explains why: live attack sessions, such as
persistent Slowloris and SSH brute-force connections, stay open much longer than the flows
seen during offline training, which pushes the rate features far below their training
range, and live cloud responses produce noticeably larger backward packets. In other words,
the live network differs from both benchmarks in distribution. This is confirmed in Figure
5, where all three domains (CIC, UNSW, and the live AWS traffic) are clearly separated from
one another.

Finally, we add a small fraction of labelled AWS traffic and recalibrate. Table 8 and
Figure 6 show that calibration helps, but only when the labelled samples resemble the
traffic being tested. When the few-shot labels cover the same attack types as the test set,
a small budget already recovers detection to a level close to a model trained entirely on
AWS. The opposite case is equally informative: in a stricter test where we calibrate only
on early BruteForce and DoS traffic and then evaluate on an unseen DDoS attack, adding more
labels actually lowers the MCC (from about 0.58 down to 0.42), because the model is tuned
toward the wrong attack regime. The practical message is simple: a few target labels
restore detection as long as they are representative of what the detector will actually
face.

4.4 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND REPRODUCIBILITY
------------------------------------------
The detector is a single XGBoost model trained with standard settings (depth 8, learning
rate 0.1, 200 trees, and 0.8 subsampling) on the nine SFM features, using MCC as the
primary metric because it remains reliable under heavy class imbalance. The model is
deliberately lightweight so that it can run on an edge sensor or a small virtual machine:
the serialized model is only a few megabytes and classifies each flow in well under a
millisecond on a single CPU core, which is more than fast enough for real-time use. We do
not apply any oversampling such as SMOTE; instead, where needed, the few-shot models simply
weight the positive class by the negative-to-positive ratio, so that the cross-network
comparison is not confounded by resampling.

All experiments follow a strict no-leakage protocol. In the cross-dataset baseline the
z-score scaler is fit on the source training data only and then applied to the target test
set, so the model never sees any target data. In joint training the scaler is fit on the
combined training data and evaluated on each held-out test set; this setting is used only
to confirm that the shared features are informative, not as a deployment scenario. In
few-shot calibration we add a small, class-balanced fraction of the target training data to
the source, fit the scaler only on this combined pool, and evaluate once on a fixed
held-out target test set that the model never sees; a zero fraction reduces to the zero-shot
case. Cross-dataset mixup follows the same held-out evaluation but builds interpolated
samples using only the target training labels. Offline cross-network results are averaged
over five seeds with 95% confidence intervals, the AWS false-alarm rates use Wilson-score
intervals, and the stricter unseen-attack AWS evaluation uses bootstrap intervals.

For the live AWS testbed, two EC2 instances (an attacker and a target/analyzer) run inside
an isolated private VPC, and traffic is captured with tcpdump and processed by NFStream to
produce the nine SFM features, with durations and rates expressed in the same units as the
CIC-trained scaler. Benign-only monitoring accumulates tens of thousands of flows for the
false-alarm measurement, while a scheduled attack session (moving through benign,
brute-force, Slowloris, and SYN-flood phases) provides the labelled attack traffic, from
which a held-out test set is reserved and never used for training. We note the limits of
this testbed for external validity: the traffic comes from a single controlled VPC with
labels derived from the execution timeline rather than from a multi-tenant production
network, the benign portion of the attack test is small (so we report MCC rather than F1),
and, as shown earlier, the benefit of few-shot calibration depends on whether the
calibration samples represent the attacks seen at test time.

4.5 DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
------------------------------
Taken together, the experiments tell a consistent story. A detector trained on a single
network collapses when moved to another, the main reason is a measurable distribution shift
between the networks rather than a lack of informative features, and a small amount of
target labels restores detection as long as those labels resemble the traffic the detector
will actually face. This pattern holds both on the offline benchmarks and on the live AWS
testbed.

The study also has clear limitations. Two of the nine SFM features are rate proxies that
are not strictly identical across the three tools, and Table 9 shows that these two features
are exactly the ones with the widest spread in UNSW, which is consistent with the direction
asymmetry observed earlier; a cleaner solution would be to re-extract all datasets with a
single flow exporter so that the rate definitions match exactly. The benefit of few-shot
calibration is also not guaranteed: as shown on the AWS testbed, it can even decrease when
the calibration labels come from attack types that differ from those at test time. Finally,
the live experiments were run in a controlled two-instance testbed rather than a diverse
production network, and we deliberately keep a single lightweight model for edge efficiency
rather than claiming it is the best possible detector; a comparison against heavier deep
domain-adaptation methods is left for future work. Adversarial robustness is likewise out
of scope here, since our preliminary results indicate that it addresses a different problem
and does not close the cross-network gap.

Table 9 reports how widely each SFM feature varies within each dataset, measured in a
shared z-score space so the two datasets are directly comparable. The feature values in
UNSW are, on average, far more spread out than in CIC, and this larger dispersion helps
explain why adapting toward UNSW recovers more fully than adapting toward CIC.

5. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
-----------------------------
This paper studied why a network intrusion detector that works well on one network often
fails on another, and how to fix it at a low cost. We showed that two datasets built with
different flow-export tools can be made comparable through a simple, statistics-based
Semantic Feature Mapping that keeps only the features that truly match across tools. Using
this shared feature space, a single lightweight model is accurate within each network but
collapses across networks, and we traced this collapse to a genuine distribution shift
between the networks rather than to a lack of useful features. The gap can then be closed
cheaply: adding only a small fraction of labelled data from the new network restores
detection to a level close to full retraining. Crucially, we confirmed the whole recipe on
a controlled cloud testbed, where the detector kept a low false-alarm rate on normal
traffic and recovered its detection ability once a few representative labels from the new
environment were provided.

For future work, a natural next step is to study the detector under attack rather than only
under normal distribution shift. This includes evasion attacks, where an adversary slightly
alters traffic to avoid detection, and adversarial training as a possible defence, so that
robustness and cross-network generalization can be examined together. Other promising
directions are continuous, online adaptation that keeps the model up to date as traffic
drifts over time, automated labelling to reduce the manual effort of calibration, extending
the detector from a simple attack-or-benign decision to recognizing specific attack
categories, and validating the approach on larger and more diverse real-world networks
beyond the controlled testbed used here.
