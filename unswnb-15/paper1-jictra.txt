TITLE
-----
Semantic Feature Mapping Validated on Live Cloud Traffic: A Deployment-Oriented
Recipe for Cross-Network Intrusion Detection


AUTHORS
-------
Hero Yudo Martono (corresponding author), Iwan Syarif, Ferry Astika Saputra

Affiliation: Department of Informatics and Computer Engineering, Politeknik
Elektronika Negeri Surabaya (PENS), Surabaya, Indonesia
Email: hero@pens.ac.id; iwanarif@pens.ac.id; ferryas@pens.ac.id


ABSTRACT
--------
Machine-learning-based network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) often perform well
within a training network but fail to generalize to other networks. One overlooked
challenge is feature-extractor mismatch, in which different flow-export tools compute
nominally equivalent features differently. This study proposes a statistically
audited Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) that combines semantic verification with
scale-distribution checks and rejects incompatible feature pairs, including
TCP-window features. Using CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15, we evaluate cross-network
generalization with a lightweight XGBoost model. Although in-domain performance
reaches Matthews correlation coefficients (MCC) of 0.91 and 0.74, single-source
cross-network performance collapses to approximately zero. Controlled experiments
show the shared features retain discriminative information under joint training,
while a domain-classifier accuracy of about 0.99 evidences substantial distribution
shift. We further evaluate the approach on real AWS traffic - a field validation step
largely absent from prior cross-dataset studies. Zero-shot transfer performs poorly,
whereas calibration with 1% of target labels raises MCC to 0.49, and 10-20% to about
0.59, comparable to the AWS-only reference (0.598). AWS gains remain dependent on the
evaluation protocol.

Keywords: network intrusion detection; cross-network generalization; semantic
feature mapping; feature-extractor mismatch; real-traffic validation; few-shot
calibration; edge deployment.


1. INTRODUCTION
---------------
Cyberattacks impose major and growing economic losses [19], making ML-based NIDS a
critical line of defense [17,18]. Such NIDS routinely report over 99% accuracy on
public benchmarks, yet this rarely materializes as a working detector on a different, previously unseen
network. The gap stems from a seldom-tested assumption: that training and
operational traffic share the same distribution. Prior cross-dataset studies
[1,2,3] confirm the collapse - average MCC falling from 0.946 within-dataset to
0.294 cross-dataset [1] - but they generally stop at observing it, and they
evaluate only dataset-to-dataset, never against live production traffic.

This paper closes two deployment-facing gaps that, to our knowledge, no prior work
addresses together:

(1) Feature-extractor mismatch. Different flow exporters compute the same nominal
feature differently; a name-based unified schema silently admits non-comparable
pairs. We detect and reject such pairs by statistical validation.

(2) No validation on a real new network. Cross-dataset papers measure
dataset-to-dataset drops; none deploys the mapped model on live traffic to measure
false-alarm behavior and repair cost.

Core novelty (what is new here). The combination that is absent from prior work: a
statistically validated cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping carried end-to-end to a
live cloud network (AWS) - measuring false-alarm rate under benign traffic,
quantifying a three-domain distribution shift, and restoring detection with a few
local labels. Unified-feature-set work [4] standardizes schemas but neither
validates each pair statistically nor tests on live traffic; cross-dataset studies
[1,2,3] document the drop but offer no field validation or low-cost repair. We
bridge both.

Contributions.
(1) A cross-tool SFM with per-pair statistical validation, including a concrete
feature-extractor mismatch finding (TCP-window features).
(2) Quantitative evidence of cross-network collapse, and a controlled diagnosis
separating feature insufficiency from distribution shift.
(3) A low-cost repair (1% target labels, or cross-dataset mixup) with multi-seed
confidence intervals.
(4) End-to-end validation on live AWS traffic: false-alarm rate, a quantified
three-domain shift, and few-shot recovery - the piece missing from prior
cross-dataset work.

Research questions. RQ1: can semantically equivalent flow features from
heterogeneous tools be reliably identified? RQ2: how far does a single-source NIDS
generalize after semantic alignment? RQ3: what distinguishes representation
insufficiency from distribution shift? RQ4: how much target labeling recovers
performance? RQ5: does the recipe transfer to real AWS traffic?

Reproducibility. All reported values come from executed experiments; no
illustrative values are presented as results, and negative/non-monotone outcomes
are reported as observed.


2. RELATED WORK
---------------
ML-based NIDS span tree ensembles and deep models (e.g., deep and transformer-based
detectors [20,21], and XGBoost variants on UNSW-NB15 [22]), most reporting strong
in-domain results that do not guarantee cross-network robustness.

Cross-dataset generalization. D'Hooge et al. [2] showed supervised NIDS fail on
unseen networks; Verkerken et al. [3] reported about 30% AUROC drop with
unsupervised models; Cantone et al. [1] documented MCC falling from 0.946 (within)
to 0.294 (cross) over four datasets. These works confirm the collapse but stop at
observing it, without separating feature insufficiency from distribution shift and
without a low-cost recovery or live-traffic validation.

Unified feature sets. Sarhan et al. [4,5] proposed a standardized NetFlow feature
set to enable cross-dataset comparison. We go further: each mapped pair is
statistically validated on its original scale, and spuriously named matches are
rejected (the TCP-window case) - exactly what a name-based schema admits silently.

Dataset integrity. Reconstructions found over 20% of CIC-IDS2017 flows needed
relabeling [6], CICFlowMeter feature errors [7,8], and 6-7% label-error rates on
CIC/CSE-CIC datasets [9]. Catillo et al. [10] questioned whether benchmark progress
reflects real advances. Layeghy et al. [11] showed synthetic benign traffic differs
statistically (large Wasserstein distance) from real networks - motivating both our
cross-tool mapping and our live-traffic validation.

Domain adaptation. CORAL [12] aligns covariance; few-shot methods target
label-limited NIDS. Deep domain-invariant NIDS learns adversarially invariant
features but needs a heavy deep stack and ample target data - impractical for edge.
We keep a fixed lightweight XGBoost [13] backbone and recover performance with cheap
per-deployment calibration, a different edge-oriented operating point. A head-to-head
against deep DA needs a different stack and is left as future work (scope stated
explicitly, not an unmeasured superiority claim).


3. DATA AND SEMANTIC FEATURE MAPPING
------------------------------------
Datasets (deliberately cross-source). CSE-CIC-IDS2018 [14] (CICFlowMeter, 68 clean
features) and UNSW-NB15 [15,16] (Argus and Bro/Zeek, 42 features; 175,341 train and
82,332 test records). The differing extraction tools are a prerequisite for
realistically testing feature-extractor mismatch, not a weakness. All attack types
are collapsed to a single attack class (controlled binary setting).

SFM construction and validation. SFM maps functionally equivalent features even when
names differ (e.g., Flow Duration <-> dur). Each candidate pair is not accepted on
name similarity but validated in three steps: (i) match semantics from each tool's
official documentation; (ii) return CIC features to original scale (see Eq. 1, as our
CIC release is z-scored); (iii) compare summary statistics (median, p99, max) for
scale-order equivalence. A pair is aligned when median/p99 ratios share an order of
magnitude, scale-mismatch at 1-3 orders (unit conversion), different-feature beyond
3 orders. The nine strong pairs form Model A; two inter-arrival-time pairs form
optional Model B; two TCP-window pairs are discarded as a feature-extractor mismatch
(continuous in CICFlowMeter, effectively binary in Argus/Bro) despite similar names.
Per-dataset z-score normalization resolves residual unit differences so each retained
pair stays semantically comparable.

Key finding (TCP-window mismatch): UNSW swin/dwin are practically binary ({0,255})
while CIC Init Fwd/Bwd Win Byts are continuous (0-65535). Despite both being named
"TCP window", the data are not comparable, so we discard the pair as a
feature-extractor mismatch. Final Model A (9 features): dur, spkts, dpkts, sbytes,
dbytes, smean, dmean, sload, dload; z-score fit on train only (no leakage).


4. CROSS-NETWORK BASELINE AND DIAGNOSIS
---------------------------------------
Collapse (RQ2). A single XGBoost excels in-domain (MCC 0.91 on CIC, 0.74 on UNSW)
but collapses cross-network (MCC approximately 0) - consistent with the literature
but here measured on the SFM-aligned feature space, ruling out a trivial schema
mismatch as the cause. We report MCC as the primary metric (Eq. 2) because it is
robust under heavy class imbalance.

[RUMUS 2 - Matthews Correlation Coefficient]
  (a) notasi terbaca:
      MCC = (TP*TN - FP*FN) / sqrt((TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN))
  (b) kode LaTeX (paste ke Equation Word, mode LaTeX):
      \mathrm{MCC} = \frac{TP\cdot TN - FP\cdot FN}{\sqrt{(TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN)}}

TABLE 2. Cross-dataset baseline (MCC, Model A). In-domain excellence collapses
cross-network (cross values indistinguishable from zero over 5 seeds).
  Scenario                        Model A    Model B
  Same-CIC (train & test CIC)     0.913      0.914
  Same-UNSW (train & test UNSW)   0.745      0.743
  CIC -> UNSW                     -0.072     0.004
  UNSW -> CIC                     -0.061     -0.046
  Generalization gap              0.81-0.99  0.79-0.91

Diagnosis: shift, not (only) insufficiency (RQ3). Joint training on the nine SFM
features performs well on both domains, showing the shared features carry sufficient
discriminative information - so feature insufficiency is not the sole explanation. A
domain classifier separates CIC from UNSW with approximately 0.99 accuracy and a
large inter-domain 1-Wasserstein distance, directly measuring the distribution shift
as the major contributor. This is conceptually motivated by the Ben-David bound on
target-domain error (Eq. 3), where a larger domain divergence upper-bounds
achievable cross-domain performance.

[RUMUS 3 - Ben-David domain-adaptation bound (motivasi)]
  (a) notasi terbaca:
      eps_T(h) <= eps_S(h) + d_HdH(S,T) + lambda
      (target error <= source error + domain divergence + ideal joint error)
  (b) kode LaTeX (paste ke Equation Word, mode LaTeX):
      \epsilon_T(h) \le \epsilon_S(h) + d_{\mathcal{H}\Delta\mathcal{H}}(S,T) + \lambda


5. LOW-COST RECOVERY (RQ4)
--------------------------
Minimal calibration recovers cross-network detection: using only 1% of
target-network labels, or supervised cross-dataset mixup that uses only target train
labels, restores cross-network MCC into the 0.65-0.91 range (multi-seed, with
confidence intervals). An adaptation-direction asymmetry is observed and analyzed:
CIC->UNSW recovers lower (about 0.66-0.69) than UNSW->CIC (about 0.90), explained by
greater domain dispersion in UNSW (mean SFM-feature variance 3.64 vs 0.68 for CIC,
about 5.3x), concentrated in rate/packet-size features. We report this as an
evidence-consistent interpretation, not a causal proof.

TABLE 3. Few-shot target adaptation (cross-network MCC; mean over 5 seeds).
  Target label fraction   CIC->UNSW   UNSW->CIC
  0% (baseline)           -0.074      -0.094
  1%                      0.659       0.900
  5%                      0.689       0.909
  10%                     0.691       0.911
  Mixup (target train)    0.680       0.798


6. REAL-TRAFFIC VALIDATION ON AWS (RQ5) - THE KEY DIFFERENTIATOR
----------------------------------------------------------------
We deploy the SFM-aligned detector on live AWS cloud traffic, the step prior
cross-dataset work omits. Three consistent findings:

(1) Stable under benign traffic. False-alarm rate stays below 0.5% without drift
(0.00% at 3 min, 0.38% at 1 h, 0.42% over 22,644 flows at 6 h), ruling out any
unit/scaling defect (which would spike FAR toward 100%).

(2) Zero-shot does not transfer. Benchmark excellence collapses on live traffic
(MCC approximately 0; -0.22 for CIC); a three-domain analysis attributes this to
genuine distribution shift (domain-classifier accuracy approximately 0.99 among
CIC/UNSW/AWS), concentrated in rate features and backward packet size.

(3) Cheaply repairable. 1% AWS labels (approximately 107 flows) lift MCC from
approximately 0 to 0.49; 10-20% reaches approximately 0.59, on par with AWS-only
(0.598). We report MCC because AWS-test is approximately 99% attack, where F1 would
be misleadingly high.

A stricter episode-level split shows the few-shot gain is smaller and
episode-dependent; we present the flow-level operating point as practical, not a
universal prescription. The most robust evidence for low-cost adaptation remains the
offline cross-network experiments (Section 5).


7. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
-----------------------------
The findings form a coherent narrative: single-source NIDS collapse cross-network;
a directly measured distribution shift (not feature insufficiency) is the major
contributor; and minimal calibration or mixup recovers performance, confirmed on
live AWS traffic. Limitations: the direction asymmetry is not fully resolved; using
a single XGBoost is a deliberate edge-efficiency choice (2.9 MB, sub-millisecond
inference) rather than a limitation

cross-network gap - confirming the two problems need different solutions.


8. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
-----------------------------
We presented a statistically validated cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping and -
uniquely among cross-dataset NIDS studies - validated the full recipe on live AWS
traffic: stable false-alarm behavior, a quantified three-domain distribution shift,
and few-shot recovery at minimal labeling cost. Future work: online/streaming
continuous adaptation with drift detection; automated few-shot labeling (active
learning, SIEM/SOC integration) with a trusted-holdout guardrail; robust ensembles
under edge budgets; and multi-class attack-category extension. Adversarial
robustness is pursued in a separate study.
