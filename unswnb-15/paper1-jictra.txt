TITLE
-----
Semantic Feature Mapping for Cross-Network Intrusion Detection: Validation on a Live Cloud Testbed

AUTHORS
-------
Hero Yudo Martono (corresponding author), Iwan Syarif, Ferry Astika Saputra

Affiliation: Department of Informatics and Computer Engineering, Politeknik
Elektronika Negeri Surabaya (PENS), Surabaya, Indonesia
Email: hero@pens.ac.id; iwanarif@pens.ac.id; ferryas@pens.ac.id

ABSTRACT
--------
Machine-learning network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) perform well within a
training network but often fail to generalize to unseen networks. One overlooked
obstacle is feature-extractor mismatch, where different flow-export tools compute
nominally equivalent features differently. We propose a statistically audited Semantic
Feature Mapping (SFM) that pairs semantic verification with unit-corrected
scale-distribution checks, rejecting incompatible pairs (e.g., TCP-window) and yielding
a nine-feature space (seven exact equivalents, two documented rate proxies). Using
CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15 with a lightweight XGBoost detector, in-domain MCC reaches
0.91 and 0.74 but single-source cross-network MCC collapses to near zero (5-seed means
-0.074 and -0.094). Joint training shows the shared features remain discriminative,
while a 0.99 domain-classifier accuracy indicates substantial distribution shift. Our
central positive result: this shift is cheaply repairable - 1% of target labels restores
cross-network MCC to 0.659 and 0.900. We then stress-test on a controlled live AWS cloud
testbed: benign false-alarm rate stays below 0.5% over six hours, and representative
few-shot calibration lifts MCC from 0.074 to 0.596 (matching the 0.598 in-domain
reference), whereas a stricter unseen-attack test shows no gain - delineating the method's
key condition: calibration labels must represent the target traffic.

Keywords: network intrusion detection; cross-network generalization; semantic
feature mapping; feature-extractor mismatch; cloud testbed validation; few-shot
calibration; edge deployment.

1. INTRODUCTION
---------------
Cyberattacks impose major and growing economic losses [1], making ML-based NIDS a
critical line of defense [2,3]. Such NIDS routinely report over 99% accuracy on
public benchmarks, yet this rarely materializes as a working detector on a different,
previously unseen network. The gap stems from a seldom-tested assumption: that
training and operational traffic share the same distribution. Prior cross-dataset
studies [4,5,6] confirm the collapse - average MCC falling from 0.946 within-dataset
to 0.294 cross-dataset [4] - but they generally stop at observing it, and they
evaluate only dataset-to-dataset, without testing on a live network testbed.

This paper targets two deployment-facing gaps that, among the works we surveyed
(Table 1), are seldom addressed together:

(1) Feature-extractor mismatch. Different flow exporters compute the same nominal
feature differently; a name-based unified schema silently admits non-comparable
pairs. We detect and reject such pairs by an exploratory, statistics-based
scale-equivalence audit, and explicitly document directional/unit proxies where exact
equivalence is not possible across schemas.

(2) No validation on a live network environment. Cross-dataset papers measure
dataset-to-dataset drops; the surveyed studies do not deploy the mapped model on a
live cloud testbed to measure false-alarm behavior and calibration conditions.

Core novelty (what is new here). The novelty is the COMBINATION of three complementary
pieces, which among the studies we surveyed (Table 1) are seldom brought together: a
statistically audited cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping carried end-to-end to a
controlled live cloud testbed (AWS) - measuring false-alarm rate under benign traffic,
quantifying a three-domain distribution shift, and evaluating when few-shot calibration
restores detection. Unified-feature-set work [7] standardizes schemas but neither
audits each pair for scale-equivalence nor tests on a live testbed; cross-dataset
studies [4,5,6] document the drop but offer no live testbed validation or low-cost
repair analysis. We bridge both.

Contributions.
(1) A cross-tool SFM with a per-pair unit-corrected exploratory scale-equivalence audit
that distinguishes exact semantic equivalents (7 pairs), documented rate proxies with
bounded definitional differences (2 pairs: src_load and dst_load), and incompatible
feature-extractor mismatches (TCP-window features).
(2) Quantitative evidence of cross-network collapse, and a controlled diagnosis
separating feature insufficiency from distribution shift.
(3) A low-cost repair (1% target labels, plus an indicative single-seed comparison with
cross-dataset mixup) evaluated with 5-seed confidence intervals.
(4) End-to-end validation on a controlled live AWS cloud testbed: benign false-alarm
rate, a quantified three-domain shift, and a two-protocol few-shot evaluation
(stratified flow-level vs unseen-attack episode-level) showing that calibration
effectiveness depends on target-label representativeness.

Research questions. RQ1: can compatible flow features (exact semantic equivalents and
documented rate proxies) from heterogeneous tools be reliably audited and separated
from extractor mismatches? RQ2: how far does a single-source NIDS generalize after SFM
alignment? RQ3: what distinguishes representation insufficiency from distribution
shift? RQ4: how much target labeling recovers performance? RQ5: under what conditions
does the recipe transfer to a live AWS cloud testbed?

Reproducibility. All reported values come from executed experiments; no
illustrative values are presented as results, and negative/non-monotone outcomes
are reported as observed.

2. RELATED WORK
---------------
ML-based NIDS span tree ensembles and deep models (e.g., deep and transformer-based
detectors [8,9], and XGBoost variants on UNSW-NB15 [10]), most reporting strong
in-domain results that do not guarantee cross-network robustness.

Cross-dataset generalization. D'Hooge et al. [5] showed supervised NIDS fail on
unseen networks; Verkerken et al. [6] reported about 30% AUROC drop with
unsupervised models; Cantone et al. [4] documented MCC falling from 0.946 (within)
to 0.294 (cross) over four datasets. These works confirm the collapse but stop at
observing it, without separating feature insufficiency from distribution shift and
without a low-cost recovery or live-testbed validation.

Unified feature sets. Sarhan et al. [7,11] proposed a standardized NetFlow feature
set to enable cross-dataset comparison. We go further: each mapped pair is audited by
a statistics-based scale-equivalence check on its original scale, and spuriously named
matches are rejected (the TCP-window case) - exactly what a name-based schema admits
silently.

Dataset integrity. Reconstructions found over 20% of CIC-IDS2017 flows needed
relabeling [12], CICFlowMeter feature errors [13,14], and 6-7% label-error rates on
CIC/CSE-CIC datasets [15]. Catillo et al. [16] questioned whether benchmark progress
reflects real advances. Layeghy et al. [17] showed synthetic benign traffic differs
statistically (large Wasserstein distance) from real networks - motivating both our
cross-tool mapping and our live cloud testbed validation.

Domain adaptation. CORAL [18] aligns covariance; few-shot methods target
label-limited NIDS. Deep domain-invariant NIDS learns adversarially invariant
features but needs a heavy deep stack and ample target data - impractical for edge.
We keep a fixed lightweight XGBoost [19] backbone and recover performance with cheap
per-deployment calibration, a different edge-oriented operating point. A head-to-head
against deep DA needs a different stack and is left as future work (scope stated
explicitly, not an unmeasured superiority claim).

Positioning against closest work (what we add). Rather than claim each component is
new, we position this work as the COMBINATION of three complementary pieces and state
precisely what we add to the nearest studies (TABLE 1). We make comparative claims
only against the studies we surveyed, not as an absolute first.

end-to-end onto a live cloud testbed (FAR, three-domain shift, few-shot repair).

3. DATA AND SEMANTIC FEATURE MAPPING
------------------------------------
Datasets (deliberately cross-source). CSE-CIC-IDS2018 [20] (CICFlowMeter, 68 clean
features) and UNSW-NB15 [21,22] (Argus and Bro/Zeek, 42 features; 175,341 train and
82,332 test records). The differing extraction tools are a prerequisite for
realistically testing feature-extractor mismatch, not a weakness. All attack types
are collapsed to a single attack class (controlled binary setting).

SFM construction: an exploratory, statistics-based equivalence audit. SFM maps
functionally compatible features across flow exporters even when column names differ
(e.g., Flow Duration <-> dur). This is an exploratory, statistics-based audit of
semantic and scale compatibility, not a formal hypothesis test. In four steps: (i)
verify each feature's semantics, directional scope, and units from the official tool
documentation; (ii) restore CIC features to their original units (Eq. 1); (iii) compute
the between-tool median and p99 ratios and, where a documented constant unit factor
applies, their unit-corrected counterparts (Eq. 2); (iv) assign a verdict with the
two-stage rule (Eq. 3). Aligned and scale-mismatch pairs are retained (residual scale
differences are later removed by per-dataset z-score), together with two documented rate
proxies where schemas lack exact counterparts; incompatible different-feature pairs are
rejected. The thresholds are decision-rule heuristics applied uniformly to all pairs and
reported in full for reproducibility; the per-pair numbers appear in Table 2.

Basis for selecting the two rate proxies (src_load and dst_load) and impact evaluation.
We do NOT claim that all nine features in Model A possess exact semantic equivalence
across CSE-CIC-IDS2018, UNSW-NB15, and the AWS NFStream extractor. Specifically, Model
A comprises SEVEN exact semantic equivalents (dur, spkts, dpkts, sbytes, dbytes, smean,
and dmean, which measure the exact same flow quantities and directions across all three
domains) plus TWO documented rate proxies that bridge schema differences between flow
exporters:
(1) Rationale and definitional scope of the two rate proxies:
- Forward/total byte-rate proxy (src_load: Flow Byts/s <-> sload): In UNSW-NB15 (sload,
  bits/s) and in our AWS NFStream extractor (src2dst_bytes / dur_s, bytes/s), src_load
  measures unidirectional FORWARD byte rate (differing only by the unit factor c = 8
  bits/byte). However, the 68-feature CSE-CIC-IDS2018 release omits a separate forward
  byte-rate column (Fwd Byts/s) and exports only total bidirectional byte rate (Flow
  Byts/s = (TotLen Fwd Pkts + TotLen Bwd Pkts) / Flow Duration in bytes/s). Because
  manually synthesizing TotLen Fwd Pkts / Flow Duration introduces division-by-zero
  instability on zero-duration flows, Flow Byts/s is mapped to sload with unit factor
  c = 8 bits/byte. While the two quantities coincide on unidirectional floods (e.g.,
  SYN/UDP floods where backward bytes are zero), Flow Byts/s includes backward bytes on
  bidirectional flows. We therefore classify Flow Byts/s <-> sload as a directional-scope
  rate proxy (scale / dir-proxy* in Table 2) rather than an identical feature across
  all three domains, and state this as an explicit methodological limitation.
- Backward rate proxy (dst_load: Bwd Pkts/s <-> dload): Conversely, CICFlowMeter and our
  AWS extractor measure backward rate in packets/s (Bwd Pkts/s and dst2src_packets /
  dur_s) and provide no native backward byte-rate column, whereas Argus/UNSW-NB15
  exports backward rate only in bits/s (dload) and provides no backward packet-rate
  column. Because backward-direction rate is essential to separate asymmetric
  request-response traffic from unidirectional floods, Bwd Pkts/s <-> dload is retained
  as a unit-coupling rate proxy (rate-proxy# in Table 2), where packets/s and bits/s are
  coupled by per-flow backward packet size rather than a constant factor c.
(2) Empirical evaluation of proxy impact (non-causal subset sensitivity): Three
experimental checks quantify the effect of retaining these two proxies. First, in
joint-model feature importance and subset ablation (k in {3, 5, 7, 9} top-ranked
features, Table 3), dst_load ranks second in discriminative importance behind fwd_bytes
(entering at k=3) while src_load ranks eighth (entering at k=9); together with the
exact-equivalence features, expanding from k=3 to k=9 lifts in-domain UNSW MCC from
0.702 to 0.744 and joint UNSW MCC from 0.646 to 0.731. Second, the feature-count
ablation (Table 3) shows that cross-network transfer performance is sensitive to
feature-subset composition: the minimal subset k=3 (fwd_bytes, dst_load, duration)
yields partial positive transfer on CIC->UNSW (+0.377; UNSW->CIC +0.065) alongside
lower in-domain and joint UNSW MCC (0.702 and 0.646), whereas expanding the subset to
k in {5, 7, 9} improves in-domain UNSW MCC (0.728 to 0.744) and joint UNSW MCC (0.719
to 0.731) but results in near-zero cross-network MCC (-0.057 to -0.009). While this
non-monotonic pattern does not by itself isolate a single causal mechanism, the fact
that dst_load is already present at k=3 (where CIC->UNSW reaches +0.377) and that
transfer is already near zero at k=5 and k=7 (before src_load is added at k=9)
indicates that cross-network collapse at k in {5, 7, 9} is not solely induced by either
rate proxy. Third, because definitional and packet-size differences amplify inter-domain
spread on rate features, Flow Byts/s <-> sload and Bwd Pkts/s <-> dload exhibit the two
largest UNSW variances in joint z-score space (9.00 and 9.50 vs 0.00 and 0.01 in CIC,
Table 9), which are subsequently mitigated once 1% target labels recalibrate the scaler
and tree thresholds (lifting MCC to 0.659 and 0.900).

Nine retained features (7 exact semantic equivalents + 2 documented rate proxies) form
Model A; two inter-arrival-time pairs form optional Model B; two TCP-window
pairs are discarded as a feature-extractor mismatch (continuous in CICFlowMeter,
effectively binary in Argus/Bro).

The audit is two-stage (Eq. 3): Stage 1 checks whether both tools measure the same
physical quantity up to a constant unit factor (or qualify as a documented rate proxy);
Stage 2 grades the remaining exact-unit pairs as aligned or scale-mismatch from their
unit-corrected ratios. Intuitively, a pair is aligned only when both the median and p99
ratios stay within one order of magnitude after unit correction, and scale-mismatch
otherwise. This resolves apparent outliers: once units are corrected, Flow Duration<->dur
and the IAT pairs - whose raw ratios look extreme purely because of us-vs-s/ms units -
fall back into a sensible range and are retained, whereas the TCP-window pairs (continuous
0-65535 vs binary {0,255}) have no valid unit mapping and are rejected. Table 2 lists the
raw and unit-corrected ratios per pair.

4. CROSS-NETWORK BASELINE AND DIAGNOSIS
---------------------------------------
Collapse (RQ2). A single XGBoost excels in-domain (MCC 0.91 on CIC, 0.74 on UNSW)
but collapses cross-network (MCC near 0, Figure 1) - consistent with the literature
but here measured on the SFM-aligned feature space, ruling out a trivial schema
mismatch as the cause. We report MCC as the primary metric (Eq. 4) because it is
robust under heavy class imbalance.

Diagnosis: shift, not (only) insufficiency (RQ3). Joint training on the nine SFM
features performs well on both domains (5-seed mean MCC 0.913 on CIC, SD 0.001, CI95%
[0.912, 0.914]; 0.730 on UNSW, SD 0.004, CI95% [0.726, 0.735]), showing the shared
features carry sufficient discriminative information - so feature insufficiency is not
the sole explanation (Figure 2). By contrast, unsupervised covariance alignment via
CORAL [18] fails to recover cross-network detection reliably across the five seeds
(CIC->UNSW mean MCC -0.118, SD 0.147, CI95% [-0.300, 0.064]; UNSW->CIC mean MCC -0.032,
SD 0.156, CI95% [-0.225, 0.162]), because matching first- and second-order moments
without target labels does not align class-conditional decision boundaries under severe
attack-composition shift. (We report TABLE 4 as single-run to isolate the Model A-vs-B
feature-set comparison, and the 5-seed means with 3-decimal CIs in TABLE 5; across
seeds, CIC->UNSW spans zero while UNSW->CIC is consistently slightly negative, and both
confirm near-zero non-functional cross-network detection.) A domain classifier separates
CIC from UNSW with approximately 0.990 accuracy (Proxy A-distance = 1.961) and a large
inter-domain 1-Wasserstein distance (mean W1 = 0.317 on unit-corrected shared z-space,
Table 7), directly measuring the distribution shift as the major contributor. This is
conceptually motivated by the Ben-David bound on target-domain error (Eq. 5),
where a larger domain divergence upper-bounds achievable cross-domain performance. A
high domain-classifier accuracy shows the two domains are easily separable but does not
by itself prove shift is the only cause; the controlled experiments indicate that
distribution shift is a MAJOR contributor to cross-network failure, although other
dataset-specific factors (differing label taxonomies, attack composition, dataset
artifacts, and the two rate-proxy approximations) may also play a role.

5. LOW-COST RECOVERY (RQ4)
--------------------------
Minimal calibration recovers cross-network detection: using only 1% of
target-network labels restores cross-network MCC into the 0.65-0.91 range across five
random seeds (Table 5 and Figure 3). Whereas unsupervised CORAL (0% target labels)
remains near zero (-0.118 on CIC->UNSW; -0.032 on UNSW->CIC), 1% target labels lifts
5-seed mean MCC from -0.074 to 0.659 (SD 0.015, CI95% [0.640, 0.677]) on CIC->UNSW and
from -0.094 to 0.900 (SD 0.002, CI95% [0.898, 0.902]) on UNSW->CIC, capturing 90.3% and
98.6% of the respective joint-training references (0.730 [0.726, 0.735] and 0.913
[0.912, 0.914]). Increasing the target budget to 5% and 10% yields diminishing
additional gains (0.689 [0.670, 0.708] and 0.691 [0.669, 0.714] on CIC->UNSW; 0.909
[0.909, 0.910] and 0.911 [0.910, 0.913] on UNSW->CIC; all CIs are reported to three
decimal places from multiseed_ci.json because the five UNSW->CIC seeds have very small
standard deviations of 0.0005-0.0015). As an auxiliary comparison, supervised
cross-dataset mixup (using target train labels) reaches 0.680 on CIC->UNSW and 0.798 on
UNSW->CIC; however, because mixup was evaluated on a single seed (seed 42) rather than
five seeds, comparisons involving mixup are indicative and more limited than the 5-seed
few-shot results. We rely directly on held-out target MCC (with 5-seed confidence
intervals) as the primary quantitative evidence of recovery, because computing
raw-feature Wasserstein shifts prior to explicit cross-extractor unit alignment is
dominated by unit-scale differences (e.g., us vs s in duration); unit-corrected
shared-z-space Wasserstein distances are instead reported in Section 6 (Table 7) to
characterize the baseline three-domain shift.

An adaptation-direction asymmetry is observed and analyzed: CIC->UNSW recovers lower
(about 0.66-0.69) than UNSW->CIC (about 0.90), explained by greater domain dispersion in
UNSW (mean SFM-feature variance 3.64 vs 0.68 for CIC, about 5.3x, Table 9), concentrated
in rate/packet-size features. We report this as an evidence-consistent interpretation,
not a causal proof.

6. LIVE CLOUD TESTBED VALIDATION ON AWS (RQ5)
---------------------------------------------
We evaluate the SFM-aligned detector on a controlled live AWS cloud testbed (two EC2
instances in ap-southeast-1 inside an isolated private VPC, capturing raw packets via
tcpdump into NFStream to extract the 9 SFM features), a live-testbed step omitted by the
surveyed cross-dataset studies (Table 1). We frame this experiment explicitly as a
controlled live cloud testbed validation rather than a broad multi-network production
deployment: all traffic is generated between two instances in a single VPC, and attack
ground truth is assigned from the scheduled execution timeline. During pipeline
engineering, we aligned each of the 9 SFM features extracted from NFStream to the
CIC-trained deployment scaler. Two alignment points matter. First, units: NFStream
reports duration in milliseconds and raw directional byte/packet counters, so we express
duration in microseconds (to match CICFlowMeter) and compute the rate features with a
per-second denominator (guarding against division by zero), applied once with no
secondary rescaling downstream. Second, directional scope: seven features (duration,
fwd_pkts, bwd_pkts, fwd_bytes, bwd_bytes, fwd_mean, bwd_mean) are defined identically
across CIC, UNSW, and AWS, whereas the two rate proxies carry the bounded differences
documented in Section 3 - notably, AWS src_load measures forward byte-rate while
CICFlowMeter Flow Byts/s is bidirectional, so src_load is not strictly identical across
all three domains (coinciding only on unidirectional floods). We treat this as a
methodological limitation when interpreting zero-shot and CIC+AWS transfer. Across the
two-phase testbed evaluation, we establish three findings:

(1) Phase 1: Initial operational stability under benign testbed traffic. Across three
cumulative monitoring windows on the live target server (3 min, 1 h, and 6 h; Table 6),
false-alarm rate stays below 0.5% without temporal drift (0.00% at 3 min over 203 flows;
0.38% at 1 h over 3,687 flows; 0.42% over 22,644 flows at 6 h, Wilson CI95% [0.34%,
0.51%]). While this 6-hour single-VPC testbed run does not represent the application
diversity of multi-network production traffic and does not by itself prove the absence
of all scaling or directional discrepancies across every feature, the observed
benign-traffic FAR supports the effectiveness of the duration- and rate-unit alignment
in the evaluated AWS testbed setup.

(2) Phase 2: Zero-shot does not transfer, explained by three-domain shift. When controlled

Inspection of the live flow distributions in our laboratory logs reveals the physical root
cause: live AWS attack sessions (notably persistent Slowloris and SSH brute-force flows
kept open across NFStream's timeout) exhibit a median flow duration of 64.03 s compared
with the offline training mean of 12.18 s, which depresses directional rate features by
three to four orders of magnitude (live src_load median 15.58 vs offline training mean
255,117; live dst_load median 0.12 vs offline training mean 15,260), while live cloud HTTP
responses produce much larger backward packet sizes (bwd_mean median 351 B on AWS vs 66 B
on CIC and 44 B on UNSW, yielding a single-feature W1 of 1.16). A three-domain shift
analysis over 1.62M CIC, 172,684 UNSW, and 44,287 live AWS flows (Table 7, Figure 4) -
computed in notebooks/21_dataset_shift.ipynb after explicit cross-extractor unit alignment

recomputing UNSW dst_load as dpkts/dur in packets/s) followed by a single pooled
StandardScaler (shared z-space) and averaging 1D Wasserstein distances
(scipy.stats.wasserstein_distance) across the 9 features - confirms that all three
domains are pairwise separable with domain-classifier accuracy >= 0.990 and Proxy
A-distance >= 1.961.

- Under the stratified 60%/40% flow-level split (Table 8A, held-out AWS-test: 7,049
  attack and 53 benign flows, where calibration samples cover all attack types present

  decline occurs because calibrating exclusively on low-rate, long-duration
  BruteForce/Slowloris flows shifts tree splits away from the high-rate unseen DDoS
  regime.
Together, these two protocols show that few-shot calibration is effective when the
calibration sample is representative of the target evaluation conditions, whereas
non-representative calibration can degrade transfer to unseen attack regimes.

6.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND REPRODUCIBILITY
------------------------------------------
Model, training, and edge efficiency profile. XGBoost (max_depth=8, learning_rate=0.1,
n_estimators=200, subsample=colsample_bytree=0.8), scikit-learn with stratified
train/test splits. Primary metric MCC (robust to class imbalance). Model A = 9 SFM
features (7 exact semantic equivalents + 2 documented rate proxies). Directly measured
on a single CPU core (1 vCPU, single-thread nthread=1, batch size = 1 over 1,980 flows;
model_efficiency.json) to reflect an edge sensor or lightweight VM budget, the
serialized 9-feature binary model occupies 2.9 MB (2,961.8 KB), achieves a per-flow
inference latency of 439.3 us mean (434.2 us median, SD 22.9 us), and sustains a
directly measured incremental single-core throughput of 2,276 flows/s. Because per-flow
tree scoring is independent across flows, multi-core throughput is estimated via linear
extrapolation at approximately 18,000-35,000 flows/s across 8-16 cores (an analytical
scaling estimate rather than a direct multi-core measurement).

Class-imbalance handling (no resampling). (i) metric: MCC throughout; (ii) cost
weighting: real-traffic few-shot models set scale_pos_weight = negative/positive
ratio; we avoid SMOTE/oversampling to keep the cross-network comparison free of
resampling confounds.

No-leakage protocol per experiment.
- Baseline (cross-dataset): z-score fitted on the SOURCE train only; applied
  (transform-only) to the target test. Model never sees target data.
- Joint training: z-score fitted on the combined (CIC+UNSW) TRAIN only; evaluated on
  each held-out test. Used only to show the shared features are sufficiently
  informative; it is not a deployment setting.
- Few-shot calibration: given source S and target split (T_train, T_test), draw a
  stratified fraction k of T_train (preserving benign/attack ratio), concatenate with
  the full source, fit the z-score scaler ONLY on this augmented pool, train, and
  evaluate ONCE on the fixed T_test. Scaler/model never see T_test; k=0 = zero-shot.
- Mixup: Beta-interpolation between flows of the two datasets using target TRAIN
  labels only; same held-out T_test (evaluated on seed 42 only).

Multi-seed and confidence intervals. Offline cross-network results report mean, SD, and
3-decimal 95% t-intervals (df=4) over 5 seeds {42, 1, 7, 123, 2024}. AWS FAR proportions
use Wilson-score 95% CIs; the stricter episode-level AWS split uses bootstrap 95% CIs
(1,000 resamples).

Label verification. On CIC, Benign=0; binary conversion yields exactly 1,348,453
normal and 274,808 attack, so the negative cross-network MCC is genuine, not a
labeling artifact.

AWS cloud testbed protocol (stated transparently). Region ap-southeast-1, 2 EC2
instances in an isolated private VPC (attacker + target/analyzer). Capture via tcpdump
-> NFStream (9 SFM features), with duration expressed in microseconds and the two rate
features computed once with a per-second denominator to match the CIC-trained scaler
(no secondary rescaling downstream; see Section 6). Benign-only monitoring accumulates up to 22,644 flows at
6 h (for FAR drift, Table 6). The attack session yields 17,753 labeled flows (17,620
attack, 133 benign) across a scheduled timeline (benign -> BruteForce -> Slowloris ->
SYN flood -> benign), labeled FROM THE TIMELINE. AWS-test split: 7,049 attack and 53
benign (held-out, never trained). LIMITATIONS OF TESTBED EXTERNAL VALIDITY: (i) traffic
is generated within a single controlled VPC (2 EC2 instances) with timeline-derived
labels rather than multi-tenant production networks; (ii) the benign sample on the
attack-session test is small (53 flows, ~99% attack), so we report MCC (not F1); and
(iii) few-shot recovery depends on whether calibration samples represent the test
attack regime (Table 8A vs 8B).

Domain-variance computation (context for TABLE 9). The per-feature variances in
TABLE 9 are computed in a SINGLE JOINT z-score space: both datasets are mapped by one
shared scaler so values are directly comparable; a variance near 0 means a feature is
tightly concentrated for that dataset in the shared space, while 9.5 means it is
widely spread. Computed over 1.62M CIC and 175,341 UNSW flows. (This is a descriptive
dispersion measure, distinct from the per-dataset-train z-score used for modeling.)

7. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
-----------------------------
The findings form a coherent narrative: single-source NIDS collapse cross-network;
a directly measured distribution shift is a major contributor (not feature
insufficiency alone, though other dataset-specific factors may also contribute); and
minimal calibration recovers performance when the calibration labels are representative
of the target environment, as evaluated across offline benchmarks and a controlled AWS
cloud testbed. Limitations:
(a) Rate-proxy definitional differences (src_load and dst_load): Model A combines seven
exact semantic equivalents with two documented rate proxies that are not strictly
identical across all three domains: (i) for src_load (Flow Byts/s <-> sload), UNSW
sload/8 and AWS src2dst_bytes/dur_s measure unidirectional forward byte/s whereas CIC
Flow Byts/s sums bidirectional byte/s (coinciding on unidirectional floods where
backward bytes are zero, but differing on bidirectional traffic); and (ii) for dst_load
(Bwd Pkts/s <-> dload), CIC and AWS measure backward packet/s whereas UNSW measures
backward bit/s (coupled by variable per-flow backward packet size). These two proxies
exhibit the largest UNSW variances in joint z-space (9.00 and 9.50, Table 9), and we
explicitly bound our feature-equivalence claims accordingly. While our non-causal
subset ablation (Section 3, Table 3) shows that transfer performance is sensitive to
feature composition - with partial positive transfer at k=3 (+0.377 on CIC->UNSW) and
near-zero transfer across k in {5, 7, 9} - future work should re-extract raw PCAPs across
all domains with a single unified exporter to enforce identical directional rate
definitions.
(b) Non-monotonicity and representativeness of few-shot calibration: On the AWS testbed,
few-shot calibration is not strictly monotonic on the flow-level split (Table 8A) and
declines on the episode-level split when calibrated solely on {BruteForce, DoS} and
tested on unseen {DDoS} (Table 8B, 0.579 -> 0.420), showing that few-shot adaptation
requires calibration samples that represent the target test regime; furthermore,
offline mixup was evaluated only on seed 42, limiting multi-seed comparisons to the
few-shot, CORAL, and baseline runs.
(c) External validity of the cloud testbed and model scope: The live AWS experiments
were conducted in a controlled two-instance single-region testbed with timeline-based
labeling rather than diverse multi-organization production traffic. Finally, the
direction asymmetry is not fully resolved, and using a single XGBoost is a deliberate
edge-efficiency choice (2.9 MB, 439.3 us measured single-core latency on 1 vCPU) rather
than a universal model claim; a head-to-head against deep domain-invariant baselines
(DI-NIDS, DANN) needs a different stack and is future work. Adversarial robustness is
deliberately scoped out: preliminary results show adversarial training improves
in-domain evasion robustness but does not close the cross-network gap - confirming the
two problems need different solutions.

8. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
-----------------------------
We presented an exploratory, statistics-based cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping (7
exact semantic equivalents and 2 documented rate proxies) and evaluated it across
offline benchmarks and a controlled live AWS cloud testbed. Single-source models
collapse cross-network (5-seed mean MCC -0.074 and -0.094) primarily due to distribution
shift (domain-classifier accuracy >= 0.990), while 1% target labels restores offline
cross-network MCC to 0.659 and 0.900. On the AWS cloud testbed, benign false-alarm rate
remains below 0.5% over 6 hours, and few-shot calibration restores detection from 0.074
to 0.490-0.596 when calibration labels are representative of the test traffic
(stratified flow-level split), whereas episode-level evaluation on unseen DDoS shows
that calibrating on non-representative attack types does not improve transfer. Future
work: re-extracting benchmark PCAPs with a unified flow exporter to eliminate rate-proxy
definitional differences; online/streaming adaptation with drift detection across
multi-tenant production networks; automated active-learning/SIEM labeling with a
trusted-holdout guardrail; and multi-class extensions. Adversarial robustness is pursued
in a separate study.
