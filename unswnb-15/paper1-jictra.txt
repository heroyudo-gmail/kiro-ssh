TITLE
-----
Semantic Feature Mapping Validated on Live Cloud Traffic: A Deployment-Oriented
Recipe for Cross-Network Intrusion Detection


AUTHORS
-------
Hero Yudo Martono (corresponding author), Iwan Syarif, Ferry Astika Saputra

Affiliation: Department of Informatics and Computer Engineering, Politeknik
Elektronika Negeri Surabaya (PENS), Surabaya, Indonesia
Email: hero@pens.ac.id; iwanarif@pens.ac.id; ferryas@pens.ac.id


ABSTRACT
--------
Machine-learning-based network intrusion detection systems (NIDS) often perform well
within a training network but fail to generalize to other networks. One overlooked
challenge is feature-extractor mismatch, in which different flow-export tools compute
nominally equivalent features differently. This study proposes a statistically
audited Semantic Feature Mapping (SFM) that combines semantic verification with
scale-distribution checks and rejects incompatible feature pairs, including
TCP-window features. Using CSE-CIC-IDS2018 and UNSW-NB15, we evaluate cross-network
generalization with a lightweight XGBoost model. Although in-domain performance
reaches Matthews correlation coefficients (MCC) of 0.91 and 0.74, single-source
cross-network performance collapses to approximately zero. Controlled experiments
show the shared features retain discriminative information under joint training,
while a domain-classifier accuracy of about 0.99 indicates the domains are highly
separable, consistent with a substantial distribution shift. We further evaluate the approach on real AWS traffic - a field validation step
rarely included among the cross-dataset NIDS studies we surveyed (Table 5). Zero-shot transfer performs poorly,
whereas calibration with 1% of target labels raises MCC to 0.49, and 10-20% to about
0.59, comparable to the AWS-only reference (0.598). AWS gains remain dependent on the
evaluation protocol.

Keywords: network intrusion detection; cross-network generalization; semantic
feature mapping; feature-extractor mismatch; real-traffic validation; few-shot
calibration; edge deployment.


1. INTRODUCTION
---------------
Cyberattacks impose major and growing economic losses [19], making ML-based NIDS a
critical line of defense [17,18]. Such NIDS routinely report over 99% accuracy on
public benchmarks, yet this rarely materializes as a working detector on a different, previously unseen
network. The gap stems from a seldom-tested assumption: that training and
operational traffic share the same distribution. Prior cross-dataset studies
[1,2,3] confirm the collapse - average MCC falling from 0.946 within-dataset to
0.294 cross-dataset [1] - but they generally stop at observing it, and they
evaluate only dataset-to-dataset, never against live production traffic.

This paper targets two deployment-facing gaps that, among the works we surveyed
(Table 5), are seldom addressed together:

(1) Feature-extractor mismatch. Different flow exporters compute the same nominal
feature differently; a name-based unified schema silently admits non-comparable
pairs. We detect and reject such pairs by a statistics-based scale-equivalence audit.

(2) No validation on a real new network. Cross-dataset papers measure
dataset-to-dataset drops; the surveyed studies do not deploy the mapped model on live traffic to measure
false-alarm behavior and repair cost.

Core novelty (what is new here). The novelty is the COMBINATION of three complementary
pieces, which among the studies we surveyed (Table 5) are seldom brought together: a
statistically audited cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping carried end-to-end to a
live cloud network (AWS) - measuring false-alarm rate under benign traffic,
quantifying a three-domain distribution shift, and restoring detection with a few
local labels. Unified-feature-set work [4] standardizes schemas but neither
audits each pair for scale-equivalence nor tests on live traffic; cross-dataset studies
[1,2,3] document the drop but offer no field validation or low-cost repair. We
bridge both.

Contributions.
(1) A cross-tool SFM with a per-pair statistics-based scale-equivalence audit, including
a concrete feature-extractor mismatch finding (TCP-window features).
(2) Quantitative evidence of cross-network collapse, and a controlled diagnosis
separating feature insufficiency from distribution shift.
(3) A low-cost repair (1% target labels, or cross-dataset mixup) with multi-seed
confidence intervals.
(4) End-to-end validation on live AWS traffic: false-alarm rate, a quantified
three-domain shift, and few-shot recovery - a step rarely reported in the
cross-dataset studies we surveyed (Table 5).

Research questions. RQ1: can semantically equivalent flow features from
heterogeneous tools be reliably identified? RQ2: how far does a single-source NIDS
generalize after semantic alignment? RQ3: what distinguishes representation
insufficiency from distribution shift? RQ4: how much target labeling recovers
performance? RQ5: does the recipe transfer to real AWS traffic?

Reproducibility. All reported values come from executed experiments; no
illustrative values are presented as results, and negative/non-monotone outcomes
are reported as observed.


2. RELATED WORK
---------------
ML-based NIDS span tree ensembles and deep models (e.g., deep and transformer-based
detectors [20,21], and XGBoost variants on UNSW-NB15 [22]), most reporting strong
in-domain results that do not guarantee cross-network robustness.

Cross-dataset generalization. D'Hooge et al. [2] showed supervised NIDS fail on
unseen networks; Verkerken et al. [3] reported about 30% AUROC drop with
unsupervised models; Cantone et al. [1] documented MCC falling from 0.946 (within)
to 0.294 (cross) over four datasets. These works confirm the collapse but stop at
observing it, without separating feature insufficiency from distribution shift and
without a low-cost recovery or live-traffic validation.

Unified feature sets. Sarhan et al. [4,5] proposed a standardized NetFlow feature
set to enable cross-dataset comparison. We go further: each mapped pair is audited by
a statistics-based scale-equivalence check on its original scale, and spuriously named
matches are rejected (the TCP-window case) - exactly what a name-based schema admits
silently.

Dataset integrity. Reconstructions found over 20% of CIC-IDS2017 flows needed
relabeling [6], CICFlowMeter feature errors [7,8], and 6-7% label-error rates on
CIC/CSE-CIC datasets [9]. Catillo et al. [10] questioned whether benchmark progress
reflects real advances. Layeghy et al. [11] showed synthetic benign traffic differs
statistically (large Wasserstein distance) from real networks - motivating both our
cross-tool mapping and our live-traffic validation.

Domain adaptation. CORAL [12] aligns covariance; few-shot methods target
label-limited NIDS. Deep domain-invariant NIDS learns adversarially invariant
features but needs a heavy deep stack and ample target data - impractical for edge.
We keep a fixed lightweight XGBoost [13] backbone and recover performance with cheap
per-deployment calibration, a different edge-oriented operating point. A head-to-head
against deep DA needs a different stack and is left as future work (scope stated
explicitly, not an unmeasured superiority claim).

Positioning against closest work (what we add). Rather than claim each component is
new, we position this work as the COMBINATION of three complementary pieces and state
precisely what we add to the nearest studies (TABLE 5). We make comparative claims
only against the studies we surveyed, not as an absolute first.

What we add, stated specifically: (i) vs unified feature sets [4,5] - we add a
statistics-based compatibility audit that rejects same-named but non-comparable pairs
(TCP-window), not only a shared schema; (ii) vs cross-dataset studies [1,2,3] - we add
a controlled diagnosis (insufficiency vs shift) and a low-label repair, not only
documenting the drop; (iii) vs domain-shift measurement [11] - we carry the recipe
end-to-end onto live cloud traffic (FAR, three-domain shift, few-shot repair).


3. DATA AND SEMANTIC FEATURE MAPPING
------------------------------------
Datasets (deliberately cross-source). CSE-CIC-IDS2018 [14] (CICFlowMeter, 68 clean
features) and UNSW-NB15 [15,16] (Argus and Bro/Zeek, 42 features; 175,341 train and
82,332 test records). The differing extraction tools are a prerequisite for
realistically testing feature-extractor mismatch, not a weakness. All attack types
are collapsed to a single attack class (controlled binary setting).

SFM construction: a statistics-based equivalence audit. SFM maps functionally
equivalent features even when names differ (e.g., Flow Duration <-> dur). This is an
EXPLORATORY, statistics-based audit of semantic and scale equivalence, NOT a formal
hypothesis test; it screens candidate pairs by order-of-magnitude agreement of robust
summary statistics. Steps: (i) match semantics from each tool's official
documentation; (ii) return CIC features to original scale (Eq. 1, as CIC release is
z-scored); (iii) on the original scale, compute the between-tool ratios of the median
and the 99th percentile (p99): r_med = med_CIC / med_UNSW and r_p99 = p99_CIC /
p99_UNSW, then classify by the order-of-magnitude rule (Eq. 2). The thresholds are
decision-rule heuristics applied uniformly to all pairs (exploratory audit, not a
confirmatory test), reported in full for reproducibility. Scale-mismatch pairs are
RETAINED (unit/scale difference resolved by per-dataset z-score); different-feature
pairs are REJECTED. One caveat applies to the two RATE features: Flow Byts/s<->sload
and Bwd Pkts/s<->dload. For sload the difference is a byte/s-vs-bit/s unit convention
(a constant factor, resolved by z-score). For Bwd Pkts/s<->dload, however, the match
is only an APPROXIMATE rate PROXY: packets/s and bits/s are not inter-convertible by a
constant (the per-flow packet size varies), so we do NOT claim exact semantic
equivalence for this pair; we retain it as a standardized rate proxy and flag it in
the Limitations. Nine retained strong pairs form Model A; two inter-arrival-time
pairs form optional Model B; two TCP-window pairs are discarded as a feature-extractor
mismatch (continuous in CICFlowMeter, effectively binary in Argus/Bro).

while CIC Init Fwd/Bwd Win Byts are continuous (0-65535). Despite both being named

dbytes, smean, dmean, sload, dload; z-score fit on train only (no leakage).


4. CROSS-NETWORK BASELINE AND DIAGNOSIS
---------------------------------------
Collapse (RQ2). A single XGBoost excels in-domain (MCC 0.91 on CIC, 0.74 on UNSW)
but collapses cross-network (MCC approximately 0) - consistent with the literature
but here measured on the SFM-aligned feature space, ruling out a trivial schema
mismatch as the cause. We report MCC as the primary metric (Eq. 3) because it is
robust under heavy class imbalance.

Diagnosis: shift, not (only) insufficiency (RQ3). Joint training on the nine SFM
features performs well on both domains, showing the shared features carry sufficient
discriminative information - so feature insufficiency is not the sole explanation. (We report TABLE 2 as single-run to isolate the Model A-vs-B feature-set comparison, and the 5-seed means with CIs in TABLE 3; the two baselines agree up to multi-seed averaging and are both indistinguishable from zero.) A
domain classifier separates CIC from UNSW with approximately 0.99 accuracy and a
large inter-domain 1-Wasserstein distance, directly measuring the distribution shift
as the major contributor. This is conceptually motivated by the Ben-David bound on
target-domain error (Eq. 4), where a larger domain divergence upper-bounds
achievable cross-domain performance. A high domain-classifier accuracy shows the two domains are easily separable but does not by itself prove shift is the only cause; the controlled experiments indicate that distribution shift is a MAJOR contributor to cross-network failure, although other dataset-specific factors (differing label taxonomies, attack composition, dataset artifacts) may also play a role.

5. LOW-COST RECOVERY (RQ4)
--------------------------
Minimal calibration recovers cross-network detection: using only 1% of
target-network labels, or supervised cross-dataset mixup that uses only target train
labels, restores cross-network MCC into the 0.65-0.91 range (multi-seed, with
confidence intervals). An adaptation-direction asymmetry is observed and analyzed:
CIC->UNSW recovers lower (about 0.66-0.69) than UNSW->CIC (about 0.90), explained by
greater domain dispersion in UNSW (mean SFM-feature variance 3.64 vs 0.68 for CIC,
about 5.3x), concentrated in rate/packet-size features. We report this as an
evidence-consistent interpretation, not a causal proof.

6. REAL-TRAFFIC VALIDATION ON AWS (RQ5) - THE KEY DIFFERENTIATOR
----------------------------------------------------------------
We deploy the SFM-aligned detector on live AWS cloud traffic, the step prior
cross-dataset work omits. Three consistent findings:

(1) Stable under benign traffic. False-alarm rate stays below 0.5% without drift
(0.00% at 3 min, 0.38% at 1 h, 0.42% over 22,644 flows at 6 h), ruling out any
unit/scaling defect (which would spike FAR toward 100%).

(2) Zero-shot does not transfer. Benchmark excellence collapses on live traffic
(MCC approximately 0; -0.22 for CIC); a three-domain analysis attributes this to
genuine distribution shift (domain-classifier accuracy approximately 0.99 among
CIC/UNSW/AWS), concentrated in rate features and backward packet size.

(3) Cheaply repairable. 1% AWS labels (approximately 107 flows) lift MCC from
approximately 0 to 0.49; 10-20% reaches approximately 0.59, on par with AWS-only
(0.598). We report MCC because AWS-test is approximately 99% attack, where F1 would
be misleadingly high.

A stricter episode-level split shows the few-shot gain is smaller and
episode-dependent; we present the flow-level operating point as practical, not a
universal prescription. The most robust evidence for low-cost adaptation remains the
offline cross-network experiments (Section 5).


6.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP AND REPRODUCIBILITY
------------------------------------------
Model and training. XGBoost (max_depth=8, learning_rate=0.1, n_estimators=200,
subsample=colsample_bytree=0.8), scikit-learn with stratified train/test splits.
Primary metric MCC (robust to class imbalance). Model A = 9 SFM features; the final
binary model is 2.9 MB (200 trees, depth 8).

Class-imbalance handling (no resampling). (i) metric: MCC throughout; (ii) cost
weighting: real-traffic few-shot models set scale_pos_weight = negative/positive
ratio; we avoid SMOTE/oversampling to keep the cross-network comparison free of
resampling confounds.

No-leakage protocol per experiment.
- Baseline (cross-dataset): z-score fitted on the SOURCE train only; applied
  (transform-only) to the target test. Model never sees target data.
- Joint training: z-score fitted on the combined (CIC+UNSW) TRAIN only; evaluated on
  each held-out test. Used only to show the shared features are sufficiently
  informative; it is not a deployment setting.
- Few-shot calibration: given source S and target split (T_train, T_test), draw a
  stratified fraction k of T_train (preserving benign/attack ratio), concatenate with
  the full source, fit the z-score scaler ONLY on this augmented pool, train, and
  evaluate ONCE on the fixed T_test. Scaler/model never see T_test; k=0 = zero-shot.
- Mixup: Beta-interpolation between flows of the two datasets using target TRAIN
  labels only; same held-out T_test.

Multi-seed and confidence intervals. Offline cross-network results report mean over
5 seeds with 95% CIs. AWS FAR proportions use Wilson-score 95% CIs; the stricter
episode-level AWS split uses bootstrap 95% CIs (1000 resamples).

Label verification. On CIC, Benign=0; binary conversion yields exactly 1,348,453
normal and 274,808 attack, so the negative cross-network MCC is genuine, not a
labeling artifact.

AWS real-traffic protocol (differentiator; stated transparently). Region
ap-southeast-1, 2 EC2 in a private VPC (attacker + target/analyzer). Capture via
tcpdump -> NFStream (9 SFM features). Benign-only monitoring accumulates up to 22,644
flows at 6 h (for FAR drift). The attack session yields 17,753 labeled flows (17,620
attack, 133 benign) across a scheduled timeline (benign -> BruteForce -> Slowloris ->
SYN flood -> benign), labeled FROM THE TIMELINE. AWS-test split: 7,049 attack and 53
benign (held-out, never trained). LIMITATION: the benign sample on the attack-session
test is small (53 flows), so AWS-test is approximately 99% attack - we therefore
report MCC (not F1) and treat AWS few-shot gains as protocol-dependent (Section 6).
The episode-level split (train {BruteForce, DoS}, test {DDoS}) uses 204 flows (121
DDoS, 83 benign).

Domain-variance computation (context for TABLE 4). The per-feature variances in
TABLE 4 are computed in a SINGLE JOINT z-score space: both datasets are mapped by one
shared scaler so values are directly comparable; a variance near 0 means a feature is
tightly concentrated for that dataset in the shared space, while 9.5 means it is
widely spread. Computed over 1.62M CIC and 175,341 UNSW flows. (This is a descriptive
dispersion measure, distinct from the per-dataset-train z-score used for modeling.)


7. DISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
-----------------------------
The findings form a coherent narrative: single-source NIDS collapse cross-network;
a directly measured distribution shift is a major contributor (not feature
insufficiency alone, though other dataset-specific factors may also contribute); and minimal calibration or mixup recovers performance, confirmed on
live AWS traffic. Limitations: (a) one SFM pair, Bwd Pkts/s<->dload, matches a PACKET-rate feature to a
BIT-rate feature; these are not inter-convertible by a constant, so it is retained as
an approximate rate proxy rather than an exact semantic equivalence - a threat to the
SFM equivalence claim that should be resolved by dropping or replacing the pair and
re-running the affected experiments in future work. (b) the direction asymmetry is not
fully resolved; using
a single XGBoost is a deliberate edge-efficiency choice (2.9 MB, sub-millisecond
inference) rather than a limitation, but a head-to-head against deep
domain-invariant baselines (DI-NIDS, DANN) needs a different stack and is future work.
Adversarial robustness is deliberately scoped out: preliminary results show adversarial
training improves in-domain evasion robustness but does not close the cross-network gap -
confirming the two problems need different solutions.

8. CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK
-----------------------------
We presented a statistically audited cross-tool Semantic Feature Mapping. Among the
cross-dataset NIDS studies we surveyed, this work is one of the few to validate the full
recipe on live AWS traffic: stable false-alarm behavior, a quantified three-domain distribution shift,
and few-shot recovery at minimal labeling cost. Future work: online/streaming
continuous adaptation with drift detection; automated few-shot labeling (active
learning, SIEM/SOC integration) with a trusted-holdout guardrail; robust ensembles
under edge budgets; and multi-class attack-category extension. Adversarial
robustness is pursued in a separate study.
